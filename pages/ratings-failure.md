## What fails
The **gatekeepers**: credit rating agencies, auditors and others whose job is to tell investors how safe something is. They don't hold your money, so their failure doesn't take it directly. It leads you, and the rules built on their opinions, to hold more risk than you think. See [[credit-risk]].

## The backstop
For investors, there is none. A rating is an opinion, not insurance. Regulators supervise the agencies, and lawsuits and settlements can follow failures, but the money goes to governments and plaintiffs, not necessarily to the investors who lost.

## Where the backstop runs out
- **Conflicts of interest.** The issuer usually pays for its own rating.
- **Ratings lag.** Downgrades often come after markets have already lost confidence.
- **Rules lean on ratings.** Many funds and banks may only hold highly rated assets, so a wrong AAA can spread a risk across the whole system.
- **Complex products** are hard to rate and easy to rate wrongly.

## Cases
- **Enron, 2001.** The energy company's debt was rated investment grade until days before its collapse. S&P cut it to junk on 28 November 2001, and Enron filed for bankruptcy days later. A US Senate committee later examined why the agencies were so slow.
- **Mortgage securities, 2007–09.** Many bonds built from risky US mortgages were rated AAA and later downgraded en masse or defaulted. S&P later agreed to pay $1.375 billion (2015) and Moody's about $864 million (2017) to settle US government claims over their pre-crisis ratings.

## What it kills
Mainly [[bonds]] and bond [[funds-and-etfs|funds]] bought on the strength of a rating, and the bond insurers that relied on them. See [[insurer-failure]]. In 2007–09 the damage spread further: banks that held AAA-rated mortgage bonds failed or needed rescue, which reached [[bank-deposits]] and sent [[shares]] down with them.

> **Key rule:** a rating is a starting point for your own judgment, not a substitute for it. A yield far above similarly rated bonds is the market disagreeing with the rating.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why don't rating agency settlements protect the investors who lost money?

**A:** Because a rating isn't a guarantee. Settlements are paid to governments and plaintiffs under the law, not as a refund to every holder of a downgraded bond.

**Q:** How can a wrong rating spread risk across the whole financial system?

**A:** Because many rules let banks and funds hold an asset only if it's highly rated. A wrong AAA lets a risky asset into portfolios that were meant to be safe.
