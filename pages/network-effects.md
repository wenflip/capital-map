## What it is
A **network effect** is when something becomes more useful the more people use it: a phone network, a language, a currency. For a coin, it's the crowd half of [[coin-value|what gives a coin value]], and it's why one of two identical copies can win. Four forces drive it:

| | The question | When it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Real users** | Are people using it, not just holding it and hoping? | Activity that is almost all trading, with little real use |
| **2. The default choice** | Does everyone expect everyone else to keep using it? | A rival becomes the expected choice and attention moves |
| **3. The momentum loop** | Do price and attention feed each other? | The loop runs backwards: a falling price drives people out |
| **4. A lasting community** | Does it hold together through crashes, hacks and splits? | A split divides users, developers and miners |

## Why it matters
Code can be copied in minutes. A crowd can't. When two chains are technically alike, the network decides which one holds its value, and it changes slowly, in both directions.

## How it works
### 1. Real users
Using a coin means paying fees, sending payments, or running programs on its chain. Holding it in the hope of a higher price isn't use. Fees paid on the chain are one of the clearest signs of use, because they cost the user something. See [[native-coins]].

### 2. The default choice
The economist Thomas Schelling showed in 1960 that people who can't talk to each other still coordinate on whatever option stands out to them all: a **focal point**. A coin can become one. People hold it partly because they expect others to, which keeps it the expected choice. A default is hard to dislodge, but it isn't permanent.

### 3. The momentum loop
George Soros called it **reflexivity** in 1987: what people believe about a price can change the very thing the price is meant to reflect. A rising coin attracts attention, then buyers, developers and businesses, which lift it further. The same loop runs in reverse. A falling price drives attention away, which removes buyers, which pushes the price down again. [[memecoins]] show both directions most sharply.

### 4. A lasting community
Communities are tested when something breaks:
- **The DAO, 2016.** A flaw in The DAO, a large program on Ethereum, let an attacker drain about $50 million of ether. On 20 July 2016 Ethereum changed its own history to return the funds. Those who refused kept the original chain, which became Ethereum Classic. One community became two, and most of it went with the change.
- **Bitcoin Cash, 2017.** A dispute over block size ended with a split on 1 August 2017. Most of the community stayed with the original.

A community that survives a split keeps the default; the side that leaves has to build a new one. See [[forks]].

## What goes wrong
- **Mistaking momentum for users.** A price rising on attention alone can fall just as fast when attention moves on.
- **Assuming the default is permanent.** Being first gives a head start, not a guarantee.
- **Counting followers instead of users.** Noise on social media isn't use on the chain.

> **Key rule:** a crowd is worth something only if it uses the coin and stays through bad times. Look for use and staying power, not just attention.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Two coins have identical code. One has many users paying fees for real transactions; the other trades heavily on exchanges but is rarely used. Which has the stronger network effect?

**A:** The first. Paying fees is costly use, which is evidence that people want the network itself. Heavy trading can be a momentum loop that reverses as soon as attention moves on.

**Q:** How can the momentum loop work against a coin?

**A:** A falling price draws attention away, which removes buyers, which pushes the price down again. The loop that lifted the coin can take it down the same way.

## Going deeper
