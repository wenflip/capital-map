## What it is
**Measure** is the branch about keeping score: how much an investment actually made, how bumpy the ride was, and how far it fell along the way. Without measurement, a lucky year looks like skill and a risky strategy looks safe until the day it isn't.

## Why it exists
Two investments that both end up 50% higher can be very different things to own. One rose steadily; the other halved, then tripled. A single return number hides that. Measures of risk exist so you can compare results fairly and, more importantly, know in advance what you might have to sit through.

## How it works
- [[returns]] — what you made: total return, after inflation, and per year.
- [[drawdown]] — the fall from a peak, and how long it takes to recover.
- [[volatility]] — how widely returns swing around their average.
- [[fat-tails]] — extremes that occur more often than a normal distribution predicts.
- [[absolute-and-relative-risk]] — turning relative statements back into absolute chances.

| Measure | Question it answers | Good for | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Return** | How much did it make? | Comparing outcomes | Says nothing about the ride |
| **Drawdown** | How far did it fall? | Knowing what you must sit through | One bad stretch, not the everyday swings |
| **Volatility** | How bumpy is it? | Comparing risk, sizing positions | Treats gains and losses alike; misses rare crashes |

Every measure depends on the **period** it covers. A fund's five-year record that starts at a market low will look far better than one starting at a peak.

## What goes wrong
- **Short records.** A few good years prove little. Many strategies look brilliant until the first bad year.
- **Cherry-picked periods.** Start and end dates can make almost anything look good.
- **Ignoring costs and inflation.** A return before fees, tax and inflation can be far from what you keep. See [[inflation]].
- **Trusting volatility as the whole story.** Some strategies earn small, steady gains and then lose heavily all at once. Their volatility looks low right up to the loss.

> **Key rule:** always ask for three numbers together: return after costs and inflation, the worst drawdown, and the period they cover.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Fund A returned 8% a year with a worst fall of 15%. Fund B returned 9% a year with a worst fall of 50%. Which is better?

**A:** It depends on whether you could hold B through a 50% fall without selling. Many people couldn't, and would lock in the loss. A slightly lower return with much smaller falls is often the better fund to actually own.

**Q:** Why does the start date of a track record matter so much?

**A:** Because returns over any period depend heavily on where it begins and ends. A record that starts just after a crash includes the whole recovery, which flatters the result.

## Going deeper
- [[sharpe-ratio]] — return per unit of volatility, used to compare strategies with different levels of risk.
- **Downside measures.** The Sortino ratio counts only the bad swings, and value at risk (VaR) estimates a loss that should be exceeded only rarely. Both try to fix volatility's habit of treating gains as risk.
