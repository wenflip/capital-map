## What it is
**Fiat** money is currency that a government declares legal tender and that can't be exchanged for a fixed amount of anything else. Nearly every national currency today is fiat.

## Why people hold it
Fiat isn't "backed" by a commodity, but it isn't backed by nothing either. Its demand comes from:
- **Taxes.** The state requires taxes to be paid in its own currency.
- **Debts.** Loans, mortgages and contracts are written in it, so borrowers must get it to repay.
- **Prices and wages.** It is the unit everyone keeps score in.
- **The central bank and the banking system** standing behind it. See [[central-bank-money]].

## Two different "cash" holdings
Cash straddles the line on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]:
- **Physical notes** are bearer and private (**Tier 2**), but exposed to theft and loss.
- **Money in the bank** is a [[bank-deposits|bank deposit]]: recorded, legible and protected by the state (**Tier 3**), but exposed to bank failure, freezes and capital controls.

## What goes wrong
- **Inflation** steadily reduces what each unit buys. After inflation, cash earning little or no interest loses value year after year. See [[inflation]].
- **Currency crises.** When trust in a government breaks, its currency can lose most of its value quickly.
- **Capital controls** can limit how much you withdraw or move abroad.
- **Against other currencies,** its value moves every day, which changes what anything priced abroad costs you. See [[exchange-rates]].

> **Key rule:** cash is the best tool for spending and short-term safety, and one of the worst for storing wealth over decades.

## How it has failed
- **Germany, 1923.** In the Weimar hyperinflation, prices rose so fast that by November 1923 one US dollar bought about 4.2 trillion marks. Savings held in marks were wiped out.
- **Zimbabwe, 2008.** Inflation peaked at an estimated tens of billions of percent a month. The country abandoned its own currency in 2009.
- **Venezuela, 2008–21.** The bolívar was redenominated three times, removing three, then five, then six zeros.
- **The slow version.** Even in stable countries, moderate inflation compounds: a currency can lose most of its buying power over a lifetime without a crisis.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why isn't the US dollar in the same position as a memecoin, when neither is backed by anything?

**A:** Because the dollar has forced demand (taxes, debts and global trade are written in it) and a central bank behind it. A memecoin has none of these. See [[hierarchy-of-money]].
