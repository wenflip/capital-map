## What it is
**Commodities** are raw materials traded in bulk, where one unit is the same as another: crude oil, natural gas, wheat, coffee, copper, gold and silver. Because a barrel of one grade of oil is interchangeable with another, they can be traded on exchanges in standard contracts.

The main groups are **energy** (oil, gas), **metals** (industrial like copper and aluminium; precious like gold and silver) and **agriculture** (grains, livestock, coffee, sugar).

## Why people trade them
- **Producers and users** lock in prices to protect their business: a farmer sells next year's crop now; an airline buys fuel ahead.
- **Investors** buy them as protection against inflation, since raw materials feed into the price of almost everything.
- **Diversification.** Commodity prices are often driven by supply shocks and weather, which don't always move with shares and bonds.

## How it works
Very few investors store oil or grain. Most exposure comes through [[futures]]: contracts to buy or sell a set amount at a set price on a future date.

Because futures expire, an investor who wants to stay invested must **roll**: sell the contract that is about to expire and buy a later one. What that costs depends on the shape of prices across dates:
- **Contango:** later contracts cost more than nearer ones. Each roll sells low and buys high, which drags on returns.
- **Backwardation:** later contracts cost less. Each roll sells high and buys low, which adds to returns.

Fictional example: the expiring contract trades at 70 and the next at 72. Rolling costs about 2.86% (2 ÷ 70), even if the spot price doesn't move. Repeated every month, that drag can be large.

So a commodity fund's return is not the same as the spot price's change. It's the price change, plus or minus the roll, plus interest earned on the cash held as collateral.

## What goes wrong
- **Price collapses** when supply jumps or demand falls. On 20 April 2020, with storage almost full, the expiring US crude oil futures contract settled below zero, at about −37 dollars a barrel: holders paid others to take the oil.
- **Roll costs** in contango can make a fund lose money while the spot price is flat.
- **No income.** A barrel of oil pays no interest or dividend; storing physical commodities costs money.
- **Leverage.** Futures need only a small deposit, so price moves are magnified. See [[leverage]].

> **Key rule:** a commodity investment's return is the price, plus or minus the roll. Check the shape of the futures curve, not just the headline price.

## Check yourself

**Q:** The oil price is unchanged over a year, but an oil futures fund lost money. What's the most likely reason?

**A:** Contango. Each time the fund rolled into the next contract, it sold the cheaper expiring one and bought a dearer later one, losing a little every roll.
