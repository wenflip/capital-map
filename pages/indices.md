## What it is
An **index** is a number that tracks the value of a basket of shares, such as the largest companies in a country. The S&P 500 tracks about 500 large US companies; the FTSE 100 tracks the 100 largest on the London Stock Exchange. An index is a measuring tool. You can't buy an index itself, only a fund that copies it. See [[funds-and-etfs]].

## Why it exists
- **To measure a market** in one number, the way a thermometer measures a room.
- **As a benchmark** for judging whether a fund manager beat the market.
- **As a recipe** for index funds, which hold the same shares in the same proportions.

## How it works
What an index measures depends on how it **weights** its companies. Fictional example with three companies:

| Company | Share price | Shares issued | Market value |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 100 | 10m | 1,000m |
| B | 50 | 40m | 2,000m |
| C | 20 | 50m | 1,000m |

Now company A's share price rises 10% and nothing else changes. How much does the index rise?
- **Market-value weighted** (most indices, such as the S&P 500): A is 25% of the total, so the index rises **2.50%**.
- **Price weighted** (the Dow Jones Industrial Average): A's price is 100 of the 170 total, so the index rises **5.88%**.
- **Equal weighted:** each company counts the same, so the index rises **3.33%**.

Same market, same move, three different answers. The weighting decides what the index rewards.

Indices are also **rebalanced**: companies are added or removed on a set schedule by rules, for example when they grow or shrink past a size threshold.

## What goes wrong
- **Concentration.** In a market-value index, the largest companies dominate. A handful of giants can drive most of the index's return, so "the whole market" can mean a few companies.
- **Price vs total return.** Most quoted index levels leave out dividends. A **total return** index adds them back, and over decades the gap is large.
- **Hidden choices.** Every index is a set of rules about what to include. Two "world" indices can hold quite different things.

> **Key rule:** an index is a set of rules, not the market itself. Check what it holds and how it weights before you treat it as "the market".

## Check yourself

**Q:** In a market-value weighted index, which company's 10% rise moves the index more: a company worth 2,000m or one worth 200m?

**A:** The 2,000m company, ten times as much. Each company counts in proportion to its market value.
