## What it is
An **inflation-linked bond** adjusts its principal in line with a consumer price index. Because the coupon is paid on the adjusted principal, both the interest and the final repayment rise with inflation. Many governments issue them.

## Why it exists
Ordinary bonds pay fixed amounts, so unexpected [[inflation]] quietly cuts what they're worth in real terms. Inflation-linked bonds pass that risk back to the issuer and lock in a **real** return instead of a nominal one.

## How it works
**Worked example (fictional figures):** a bond with a principal of 1,000 and a 1% real coupon. Prices rise 3% over the year.
- The principal is adjusted to **1,030**.
- The coupon is 1% of 1,030: **10.30**.
- At maturity, the adjusted principal is repaid.

**Breakeven inflation.** The gap between an ordinary government bond's yield and an inflation-linked bond's yield of the same maturity shows the inflation rate the market expects. If inflation turns out higher than that, the inflation-linked bond does better.

## What goes wrong
- **Rate risk still applies.** If *real* interest rates rise, inflation-linked bond prices fall like any other bond's.
- **The index may not match your costs.** It tracks an average basket, not your own spending.
- **Deflation.** Payments can shrink when prices fall, though many issuers guarantee at least the original principal at maturity.

> **Key rule:** inflation-linked bonds protect against inflation, not against changes in interest rates.

## Check yourself

**Q:** An ordinary ten-year government bond yields 4% and an inflation-linked one of the same maturity yields 1.5%. Roughly what inflation rate is the market expecting?

**A:** About 2.5% a year — the difference between the two yields, called breakeven inflation.
