## What it is
Listed companies must publish their results regularly. In many markets that means every quarter; in others every half year. Because most companies share the same reporting periods, their results arrive in a cluster over a few weeks. That cluster is **earnings season**, and it brings one of the busiest, jumpiest stretches of the market's year.

## Why it matters
Results are where expectations meet reality. A share price is a bet on future profits; each report is a check on that bet. Prices often move more in the day after results than in the rest of the quarter. See [[priced-in]].

## How it works
**What's in a report.** Sales, costs and profit for the period, often given as **earnings per share**, plus the balance sheet and cash flow. See [[financial-statements]].

**Guidance often matters more than results.** Many companies also say what they expect next: sales, profit, spending. Because prices look ahead, a strong past quarter with weak guidance can send the price down.

**Beats and misses.** Results are compared with the consensus of analysts' forecasts.

**Fictional example.** The consensus expects 1.20 per share. The company reports 1.25: a "beat" of 0.05, about **4.2%** above forecast. Whether the price rises depends on what else is in the report, and on whether investors had quietly expected more than the published forecast.

**Reports land outside trading hours.** Many companies publish before the market opens or after it closes. So prices often **gap**: they open far from where they closed, with no trading in between. See [[exchanges]].

## What goes wrong
- **Reading too much into one quarter.** A single report is a small window on a long-lived business.
- **Short-termism.** Pressure to hit each quarter's number can push companies to cut useful spending, or to manage expectations so results land just above them.
- **Orders filled far from the last price.** A gap at the open can fill an order to sell at a much worse price than the last close.

> **Key rule:** a report moves the price by how it compares with expectations, and by what it says about the future, more than by the past numbers themselves.

## Check yourself

**Q:** The consensus is 0.80 per share. A company reports 0.76. By how much did it miss, as a share of the forecast?

**A:** It missed by 0.04, which is **5%** of 0.80.
