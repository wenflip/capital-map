## What it is
**Real estate** is land and the buildings on it: the home you live in, flats you rent out, offices, shops and warehouses. Ownership is recorded in a public **title registry**, which is what makes it the most legible store of wealth on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]].

## Why people own it
- **Use.** A home is both an asset and a place to live; owning it replaces rent.
- **Income.** Rented property can pay a regular income.
- **Leverage.** Property is the one asset most people can buy mostly with borrowed money, through a **mortgage**.
- **Inflation.** Rents and building costs tend to rise with prices over long periods.

## How it works
**Leverage cuts both ways.** A buyer puts down a deposit and borrows the rest. Gains and losses are on the whole price, but they land on the deposit.

| Fictional example | Price | Loan | Your equity | Change on your deposit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| You buy | 400,000 | 320,000 | 80,000 | — |
| Price rises 10% | 440,000 | 320,000 | 120,000 | **+50%** |
| Price falls 10% | 360,000 | 320,000 | 40,000 | **−50%** |
| Price falls 20% | 320,000 | 320,000 | 0 | **−100%** |

A 20% deposit turns a 10% price move into a 50% move on your money. That is before buying costs, interest, repairs and tax, which all reduce the gain or deepen the loss. See [[leverage]].

**Costs of owning:** purchase taxes and fees, mortgage interest, maintenance, insurance, property taxes and, for landlords, empty months between tenants.

## What goes wrong
- **Immobility**, the killer on the Cartography of Wealth. The one asset that can't move can be taxed, rent-controlled, rezoned or compulsorily purchased where it stands.
- **Price falls with a mortgage.** In the 2008 crisis, US house prices fell by roughly a quarter to a third nationally, depending on the index, and many owners ended up owing more than their homes were worth.
- **Slow sales.** Selling takes weeks to months and costs a few percent of the price.
- **Concentration.** One property is one location, one local economy and often most of a household's wealth.

> **Key rule:** property's risk is mostly in the loan. Know what a 20% price fall would do to your deposit before you buy.

## How it has failed
- **Japan after 1990.** When Japan's property bubble burst in the early 1990s, commercial land prices in its big cities kept falling until the mid-2000s.
- **The US, 2006–12.** House prices fell by roughly a quarter to a third nationally, and millions of homes were lost to foreclosure.
- **The state's claim never stops.** Property taxes are due every year whatever the market does, and land can be compulsorily purchased, rezoned or seized, because it can't be moved.

## Check yourself

**Q:** With a 25% deposit, roughly what happens to your equity if the property's price falls 25%?

**A:** It is wiped out. The price has fallen by the whole deposit, so you owe as much as the home is worth, before any costs.
