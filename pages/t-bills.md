## What it is
A **Treasury bill** (T-bill) is a short loan to a government, usually for a year or less. It pays no coupon. Instead it is sold for less than its face value and repaid at full face value; the difference is the interest.

## Why it matters
T-bills are about as close to cash as an investment gets: short, issued by a government that borrows in its own currency, and easy to sell. That makes their rate the benchmark **risk-free rate** that other returns are measured against. Money market funds, banks, central banks and many [[stablecoins|stablecoin]] issuers hold large amounts of them.

## How it works
**Worked example (fictional figures):** a one-year bill with a face value of 10,000 sells for 9,800. At maturity you receive 10,000.
- Interest earned: 200.
- Return: 200 ÷ 9,800 ≈ **2.04%** for the year.

Because they're so short, T-bill prices barely move when interest rates change. Their yield follows the central bank's policy rate closely.

## What goes wrong
- **Inflation.** A T-bill can't lose much in money terms, but if inflation runs above its yield, its real return is negative. See [[inflation]].
- **Government default.** Rare for governments that borrow in their own currency, but not impossible, and political standoffs over borrowing limits have unsettled bill markets.
- **Rolling over.** Rates on new bills change every time old ones mature, so income isn't locked in.

> **Key rule:** T-bills protect you from price swings and most default risk, not from inflation or from falling rates when you reinvest.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why does a T-bill's price barely change when interest rates rise, while a 30-year bond's price can fall sharply?

**A:** Because the T-bill matures within months, so you soon get your money back to reinvest at the new rate. A 30-year bond locks in the old rate for decades, so its price must fall to compete with new bonds.
