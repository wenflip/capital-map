## What it is
The final backstop behind a banking system is its **government**. But banks also hold large amounts of that same government's debt. This two-way dependence is often called the **bank–government loop**, or, in a crisis, the "doom loop".

## Why it matters
When the loop runs the wrong way, trouble at one end feeds the other:
1. Banks make losses, so the government promises to rescue them.
2. The promise adds to government debt, so its bonds fall in value.
3. Banks holding those bonds make further losses.
4. The government looks weaker still, and so on.

## How it works
Two things decide whether a government can be the last link:
- **The size of the banking system against the economy.** A banking system several times larger than the country's annual output can be too big to rescue, as Iceland found in 2008.
- **The government's own borrowing power.** A government that borrows in its own currency, with a central bank behind it, can usually raise money in a crisis. One that can't — because it shares a currency, or borrows in someone else's, or has already lost markets' trust — may be unable to. Cyprus in 2013 and Lebanon from 2019 are examples.

The euro area's debt crisis from 2010 is the textbook case: weak banks and weak governments in several countries pulled each other down until European institutions stepped in.

## What goes wrong
- **"Safe" government bonds aren't safe from this.** Banks are allowed to treat their own government's debt as very low risk, which concentrates the loop.
- **Rescues move the problem**, from bank balance sheets to public ones.
- **When the government can't help**, depositors can face frozen accounts or capital controls, as in Lebanon.

> **Key rule:** the government is the last link only if it is bigger and stronger than its banks. Check the size of the banking system against the economy, and whether the government can borrow in its own currency.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why can a government that borrows in its own currency usually stand behind its banks more credibly than one that can't?

**A:** Because its own central bank can create the currency its debts are owed in, so it can always raise money in that currency — though doing so can bring inflation. A government without that option can simply run out of money.
