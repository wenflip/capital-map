## What it is
**Fixed income** means lending money in return for a promise: regular interest, and your money back on a set date. The borrower might be a government, a company or a city. The promise is written in a **bond** (or, for short loans to governments, a **bill**), and most bonds can be bought and sold before they are repaid.

## Why it exists
Governments and companies need to borrow for long periods; savers want a predictable income. Bonds connect the two, and because they can be traded, a lender doesn't have to wait years to get money back. Bond markets also set the interest rates that ripple into mortgages, loans and the value of every other asset.

In the [[hierarchy-of-money|hierarchy of money]], bonds sit just above money itself: a promise to pay money in the future.

| Risk | What it is | Hits hardest |
|---|---|---|
| **Interest-rate risk** | Prices fall when rates rise | Long-dated bonds |
| **Credit risk** | The borrower can't pay in full | Weak companies and governments |
| **Inflation risk** | Fixed payments buy less | Long-dated, fixed-rate bonds |
| **Liquidity risk** | Hard to sell without a big discount | Small or unusual issues |

## What goes wrong
- **Rising rates.** A safe government bond can still lose a lot of value before it matures if rates jump. This is what sank Silicon Valley Bank in 2023. See [[bank-capital-and-liquidity]].
- **Default.** The killer on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]: a bond is only someone else's promise to pay.
- **Inflation** quietly eats fixed payments, which is why [[inflation-linked-bonds]] exist.
- **Reaching for yield.** Higher yields usually mean higher risk; a bond paying far more than government debt is telling you something.

> **Key rule:** a bond's return is fixed only if you hold it to the end and the borrower pays. Before then, its price moves with interest rates and with trust in the borrower.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You own a government bond that will certainly be repaid in full in ten years. Can you still lose money on it?

**A:** Yes, if you sell before maturity. If interest rates rise, new bonds pay more, so yours is worth less on the market. Held to maturity, you get the promised payments, but they may buy less after inflation.

**Q:** Why do riskier borrowers have to pay higher interest?

**A:** Because lenders need to be paid for the chance of not getting their money back. The extra yield over government debt is the market's price for that risk.

## Going deeper
- **Duration.** A single number estimates how sensitive a bond's price is to a change in interest rates — roughly the percentage price change for a one-percentage-point move. See [[duration]].
- **Convexity.** That estimate is a straight line; real prices curve, falling a little less than predicted when rates rise and rising a little more when rates fall.
