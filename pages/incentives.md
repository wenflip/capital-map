## What it is
Incentives are the rewards and penalties that follow from an action. People, firms and institutions tend to do more of what is rewarded and less of what is penalised, even when the stated goal is different.

## Why it matters
Advice, research, products and rules are shaped by the incentives of the people who produce them. A recommendation that benefits the recommender can differ from one that benefits the recipient. Understanding the incentives explains much of what is sold, reported and regulated without needing to assume bad faith.

## How it works
Ask who gains and who loses if the action is taken, and how large and certain those gains and losses are. Fees that rise with activity reward activity. Bonuses tied to short-term results reward short-term results. Rules that are costly to enforce are enforced less. The same person can face conflicting incentives across different roles. The strongest and most immediate incentive usually dominates.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** an adviser is paid 1% of assets each year plus a share of trading commissions. Recommending a fund that generates more trades raises the adviser's income even if it lowers the client's net return. The incentive points one way; the client's interest points another.

## What goes wrong
- **Listening only to stated motives.** What people say they optimise for can differ from what their pay and penalties reward.
- **Assuming incentives are aligned.** Overlapping interests do not mean identical interests.
- **Ignoring second-order incentives.** A rule intended to reduce one behaviour can reward a different behaviour that has the same effect.

> **Key rule:** before acting on advice or a product, ask who gains if you follow it and how that gain is earned.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A research report recommends a share. The firm that wrote it also earns fees from companies it covers. What question follows?

**A:** Whether the recommendation would be the same if the firm earned nothing from the companies it covers. The incentive does not prove the view is wrong, but it changes how much weight to give it.

**Q:** A bonus is paid on this year's profit. What behaviour does that reward?

**A:** Actions that raise this year's profit, even if they reduce profit in later years or increase risk that appears later.
