## What it is
Financial products have hundreds of names, but every return comes from supplying something in exchange. Ask **what am I supplying, and what does it earn?** and there are only four answers:

| Way | What you supply | What it earns | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Labour** | Your time, effort and skill | A wage | Pay from a job, contract work, a trade, a founder's early unpaid work |
| **2. Lending** | The use of something you own, which you get back | Interest or rent | [[t-bills\|T-bills]], [[bonds]] and [[bank-deposits\|deposits]]; rent on [[real-estate\|property]]; leasing equipment; royalties |
| **3. Ownership** | Capital committed for whatever is left after everyone else is paid | Profit | [[shares]], a [[private-business\|business]], a partnership stake |
| **4. Price and risk** | A view on a price, or a willingness to carry someone else's risk | A gain, or a premium | Trading, arbitrage, [[futures]] and [[options]], writing insurance, making markets |

Money that arrives with nothing supplied in exchange is a **transfer**, not a return: gifts, inheritance, benefits, prizes, and theft or fraud. It's real money, but none of the four earned it.

## Why it matters
- **It cuts through names.** A "high-income fund", a "structured note" and a "property income product" are each some mix of the four. Find the mix and you know where the return comes from, and what can stop it.
- **Each way carries its own risk.** A lender is paid early and capped. An owner is paid last and uncapped. A price-taker is paid only if the price moves the right way.
- **The first split is debt against equity.** Lending makes you a creditor with a fixed claim; owning makes you the owner of whatever is left. See [[claims-and-liabilities]].

## How it works
**Classify the mechanism, not the asset.** The same thing can earn in more than one way, depending on how you hold it:

| Asset | Labour | Lending | Ownership | Price and risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A business you run | Your pay for running it | — | The profit | Selling it for more than it cost to build |
| A house | — | Rent from a tenant | — | Selling for more than you paid |
| A bond | — | The coupon | — | Selling before maturity at a higher price |
| A share | — | — | Its slice of the company's profit, paid out or reinvested | Selling to someone for more |
| [[gold\|Gold]] | — | Lending it out for a fee | — | A higher price later |

**Price and risk is two different things:**
- **Pure price bets.** You buy because you expect a higher price later, or sell short because you expect a lower one. Before costs, one side's gain is the other side's loss: the trade adds nothing that can be shared out.
- **Being paid to carry risk.** An insurer, an options writer or a market maker is paid a premium or a spread to take on a risk someone else wants to get rid of. The premium arrives steadily, and the loss arrives when the risk does.

**The pecking order.** When a business pays out, workers and suppliers come first, then lenders, then owners take what's left. That order is why lending is safer and capped, and ownership riskier and open-ended.

**Worked example (fictional figures).** You buy a flat for 200,000 and let it for five years. Rent after costs is 9,000 a year, and you sell for 230,000.
- From lending (rent): 5 × 9,000 = **45,000**
- From price: 230,000 − 200,000 = **30,000**
- In total: **75,000**, with 60% from letting and 40% from the price.

Had you sold for 190,000 instead, the letting would still have earned 45,000, and the price leg would have lost 10,000. The two ways carry different risks even inside one asset. (This ignores tax and the [[time-value-of-money|time value of money]].)

## What goes wrong
- **Counting a price rise as income.** A house that rose in value hasn't paid you rent, and the rise can reverse.
- **Calling a price bet an investment.** If the only way to win is to sell to someone at a higher price, it's the fourth way, whatever the label says.
- **Collecting premiums without seeing the risk.** Selling insurance or options pays small and steady, until the event it insures happens.
- **Forgetting that labour is most people's biggest return.** Over a working life, wages usually earn more than all the other three put together. See [[human-capital]].

> **Key rule:** for any return, ask what you're supplying. If you can't name it, you're either carrying a risk you can't see or relying on a transfer.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A fund pays a steady 8% a year "income" from selling options. Which of the four ways is that, and what's the catch?

**A:** The fourth: being paid to carry risk. The premiums arrive steadily, but when markets move sharply the options it sold can cost far more than all the premiums it collected.

**Q:** You bought a flat for 150,000, earned 6,000 a year in rent after costs for four years, and sold it for 140,000. What did each way earn, and what was the total?

**A:** Lending earned 4 × 6,000 = 24,000. The price leg lost 150,000 − 140,000 = 10,000. In total you made 14,000, all of it from letting.

## Going deeper
- **Where the four come from.** Adam Smith named three original sources of all revenue in 1776: wages, profit and rent. He treated interest as a share of the borrower's profit. This page puts rent and interest together as lending, since both are paid for the use of something you get back. It also adds a fourth way, gains from price and from carrying risk, which matters far more in modern markets than it did in his.
- **Risk premia.** Much of the long-run return on an asset class can be read as a premium for carrying a risk others would rather not hold. That's the second half of the fourth way, built into the other three. See [[risk-and-return]].
