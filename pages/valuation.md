## What it is
**Valuation** is estimating what a share or a whole business is worth, so you can compare it with the price. There are two broad approaches:
- **Intrinsic:** value the cash the business will pay out in future, brought back to today. See [[time-value-of-money]].
- **Relative:** compare the price with similar companies, using ratios such as price to earnings.

## Why it exists
Price is what you pay; value is what you get. A wonderful company bought at too high a price can still be a poor investment, and a dull one bought cheaply can be a good one. Valuation is how investors decide which is which.

## How it works
**Intrinsic: the dividend growth model.** If a share is expected to pay a dividend of 2.00 next year, growing 3% a year forever, and investors want an 8% return, its value is:

> **Value = next dividend ÷ (required return − growth)** = 2.00 ÷ (0.08 − 0.03) = **40.00**

Small changes to the assumptions move the answer a lot:

| Required return | Growth | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 8% | 3% | 40.00 |
| 8% | 4% | 50.00 |
| 9% | 3% | 33.33 |

One percentage point of growth adds a quarter to the value. The formula is only as good as its guesses. Analysts use the same idea with a company's free cash flow instead of dividends; this is called a **discounted cash flow** (DCF) model.

**Relative: price to earnings.** If the share trades at 40 and the company earned 2.50 per share last year, its **P/E ratio** is 16: you pay 16 times one year's earnings. Flip it over and the **earnings yield** is 6.25%, which you can compare with bond yields.

A high P/E means the market expects strong growth; a low one means it expects little, or sees risk.

## What goes wrong
- **Garbage in, garbage out.** A valuation built on optimistic growth gives an optimistic answer.
- **Cheap for a reason.** A low P/E can mean the company's earnings are about to fall.
- **Comparing unlike things.** Ratios only work across similar companies, in similar conditions.
- **Rates change everything.** When interest rates rise, investors demand higher returns, and the value of distant future cash falls. See [[bond-pricing]].

> **Key rule:** a valuation is a set of assumptions with a number at the end. Test how much the answer moves when the assumptions change.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Using the model above, what is a share worth if next year's dividend is 3.00, growth is 2% and the required return is 8%?

**A:** 3.00 ÷ (0.08 − 0.02) = 50.00.
