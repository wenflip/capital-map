## What fails
The **real value** of savings held as cash, deposits and government bonds. Nothing defaults. You're paid every coupon and every penny back, but in money that buys less each year. **Financial repression** is the economists' term for the policies that make this happen: keeping interest rates below inflation, and steering savers and banks into government debt.

## How it works
If inflation is 5% and your savings earn 2%, you lose about 3% of your buying power each year. The government's debt shrinks against the economy by the same mechanism. The usual tools:
- **Caps on interest rates**, by law or by the central bank.
- **Rules that push banks, pensions and insurers to hold government bonds.**
- **Limits on moving money abroad**, so savers can't escape. See [[capital-controls]].


## The backstop
There's no rescue, because nothing has formally failed. What helps is owning things that keep pace with prices: [[inflation-linked-bonds]], [[real-assets]], and businesses that can raise their prices. None of these is perfect.

## Where the backstop runs out
- **It's slow and easy to miss.** A few percent a year doesn't trigger alarms, but it compounds. See [[compounding]].
- **"Safe" assets are the target.** The savings people think are safest are exactly the ones being diluted.
- **Inflation-linked bonds may be scarce,** and their index may not match your own costs.

## Cases
- **The US rate peg, 1942–1951.** From 1942 the Federal Reserve pegged short-term Treasury bills at 3/8% and capped long-term bonds at 2.5%, holding borrowing costs down while wartime and post-war inflation ran higher. The peg ended with the Treasury–Fed Accord of March 1951.
- **Advanced economies, 1945–1980.** Carmen Reinhart and M. Belen Sbrancia found that real interest rates were negative roughly half the time in their sample. For the US and UK they estimate that this reduced government debt by about 2–3% of GDP a year.
- **The 1970s.** High inflation with interest rates often below it left holders of long-dated bonds repaid in money worth far less. See [[bonds]].

**The debate.** Economists disagree about how much of the post-war fall in debt came from repression rather than strong growth, and about whether such policies are a hidden tax or sensible crisis management. The term describes a mechanism; it isn't a verdict.

## What it kills
[[cash-and-fiat]], [[bank-deposits]] and fixed-rate [[bonds]] most of all, slowly. [[real-estate]], [[shares]], [[gold]] and other real assets are less exposed, though they have their own killers.

> **Key rule:** a default you don't notice is still a default. Check your savings' return after inflation, not before.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Your deposit pays 1% while inflation runs at 4% for ten years. Roughly what happens to its buying power?

**A:** It falls by about 3% a year, so after ten years it buys about a quarter less, even though the bank paid every penny of interest.

**Q:** Why does financial repression often come with capital controls?

**A:** Because savers would otherwise move their money abroad to earn a real return, and the policy only works if they stay.
