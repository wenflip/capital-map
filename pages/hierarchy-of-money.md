## What it is
A way of ranking forms of money by **how final they are**. Each layer is a promise to pay in the layer beneath it.

| Layer | Example | Whose promise? |
|---|---|---|
| Base | [[gold]] | Nobody's — final in itself |
| Central bank money | Notes, bank reserves ([[central-bank-money]]) | The central bank's |
| Bank money | [[bank-deposits]] | A commercial bank's, payable in central bank money |
| Offshore and private dollars | [[eurodollars]], [[stablecoins]] | A foreign bank or a company, payable in dollars |
| Securities | [[t-bills]], [[bonds]] | A borrower's, payable in future money |
| Risk assets | [[shares]], [[real-estate]], crypto | Priced in money, but not a promise of money |


![An upside-down pyramid of six layers. From the top: shares, property and crypto; bonds and T-bills; offshore and private dollars; bank deposits; central bank money; and gold at the base. Each layer is a promise to pay in the layer below.](assets/figures/hierarchy-of-money-layers.svg "The same six layers as the table, from risk assets at the top to gold at the base.")

The economist Perry Mehrling describes the modern system this way. The investor John Exter drew it as an **inverted pyramid**: a small base of gold at the bottom, with ever larger layers of credit stacked on top.

## Why it matters
- **In good times** the layers look interchangeable. A deposit, a stablecoin and a T-bill all feel like "a dollar".
- **In a crisis** people sell the upper layers to get the lower ones. Bank runs, stablecoin de-pegs and "dash for cash" episodes are all moves down the pyramid.
- **Elasticity at the top, discipline at the base.** Upper layers can expand quickly through lending; the base is scarce. That is why credit booms and busts happen in the upper layers.

## Where the US dollar sits
The dollar and a memecoin are both "fiat" in the narrow sense: neither can be redeemed for anything. But they sit in very different places:
- Dollars are the **central bank's liability**, sit near the base of the global hierarchy, and have **forced demand**: US taxes must be paid in them, and trillions of dollars of debt worldwide must be repaid in them.
- A memecoin is **nobody's liability**, has no cash flows, no tax demand and no lender of last resort. Its value rests on attention alone.

So the useful question is not "is it backed?" but "how far from the base is it, and what forces people to hold it?" See [[claims-and-liabilities]].

> **Key rule:** the lower the layer, the fewer things can go wrong with it — but the less it tends to pay.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A stablecoin trades below one dollar during a market panic. In hierarchy terms, what is happening?

**A:** Holders doubt the issuer can pay one dollar per coin, so they sell the private promise (upper layer) to get dollars (lower layer). The price gap is the market's discount on that promise.

## Going deeper
- The hierarchy is not fixed. Gold was the formal base until the early 1970s; since then central bank money has been the base for most purposes, with gold held alongside it by central banks.
- Central banks defend the hierarchy by standing ready to swap upper layers for their own money in a crisis (lender of last resort, swap lines). When they do, upper layers hold their value; when they don't, they break.
