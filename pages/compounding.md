## What it is
**Compounding** means each period's return is added to the balance, so the next period's return is earned on a bigger amount. Growth builds on growth.

## Why it matters
Compounding is why the length of time money stays invested often matters more than the exact moment it goes in, and why small differences in rates — including fees — become large over decades.

## How it works
**Simple versus compound (fictional figures):** 1,000 at 5% a year for 10 years.

| Method | Interest is earned on | Value after 10 years |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | The original 1,000 only | 1,500.00 |
| Compound | The growing balance | 1,628.89 |

**Small differences grow large.** 10,000 for 30 years:
- at 7% a year it grows to about **76,122.55**;
- at 6% a year (the same return less a 1% annual fee) it grows to about **57,434.91**.

The fee looks like 1%, but it removes about **a quarter** of the final value.


![A line chart of $10,000 growing at 7% a year for 30 years. With no fee it reaches $76,123; with a 1% yearly fee it reaches $57,435. The $18,688 gap is about a quarter of the no-fee result.](assets/figures/compounding-fee-drag.svg "The same 10,000 for 30 years, at 7% a year and at 6% (7% less a 1% fee). Fictional figures.")

**The rule of 72.** Divide 72 by the percentage rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 6%, 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years; the exact answer is about 11.9. It's a quick check, and it is most accurate for rates between roughly 4% and 12%.

**Compounding frequency.** The more often returns are added, the higher the effective yearly rate. A 12% rate added monthly works out to about 12.68% a year.

## What goes wrong
- **Compounding works on costs too:** fees, inflation and interest on debt all compound against you.
- **Losses interrupt it.** A large fall shrinks the base that future returns grow from. See [[risk-and-return]].
- **Taking money out early** stops the later, largest years of growth from happening.

> **Key rule:** in compounding, the last years do most of the work — so time in the market, low costs and avoiding large losses matter more than clever timing.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Using the rule of 72, roughly how long does money take to double at 9% a year?

**A:** About 8 years (72 ÷ 9). The exact figure is a little over 8.

**Q:** Why does a 1% annual fee cost far more than 1% of the final value over 30 years?

**A:** Because each year's fee also removes the future returns that money would have earned. Over 30 years at these rates it removes about a quarter of the final value.
