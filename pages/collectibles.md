## What it is
**Collectibles** are objects people buy partly for pleasure and partly as a store of value: art, watches, classic cars, rare wine and whisky, coins, stamps, gems, trading cards. Their value isn't set by income or by a formula. It's what the next buyer is willing to pay.

## Why people own them
- **Enjoyment.** You can wear the watch, drive the car or hang the painting.
- **Scarcity.** The supply of a particular artist's work or a discontinued model is fixed.
- **Privacy and portability.** Many collectibles aren't recorded in a public register, and small ones can be carried. That is why they sit near the private, bearer end of the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]], though large sales leave records.

## How it works
Prices are set in **thin markets**: few buyers and sellers, and each object slightly different. Sales happen through auction houses, dealers and private deals. Fees are high: auction houses charge both the buyer and the seller, and together the fees can take a large slice of the price.

Collectibles pay **no income**, and holding them costs money: insurance, secure storage, climate control for wine and art, and proof that the object is genuine (**provenance**).

## What goes wrong
- **Illiquidity and subjective value**, the killer on the Cartography of Wealth. When you are forced to sell, the price is one buyer's word on one day.
- **Fashion.** Tastes change. A category that was hot for a decade can fall out of favour.
- **Fakes and disputes.** A forged signature or a gap in the ownership history can destroy the value.
- **Damage, theft and loss**, with no one to claim against except your insurer.
- **Price indices flatter.** Published returns often track only items that sold at auction, which leaves out the pieces that didn't sell.

> **Key rule:** buy a collectible because you value owning it. Treat any gain as a bonus, because selling is slow, expensive and depends on taste.

## How it has failed
- **Forged art.** The Knoedler gallery in New York, one of the oldest in the US, closed abruptly in 2011 after it emerged that it had sold about 40 forged paintings, part of a ring whose fakes sold for over $80 million.
- **Fake wine.** In 2013 a US court convicted the collector Rudy Kurniawan of selling counterfeit rare wines, many of which had passed through major auctions.
- **Fads.** Categories that boomed, from some toys to some trading cards, have lost most of their value when collectors moved on.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why can two identical-looking watches sell for very different prices?

**A:** Because collectibles are valued one by one. Condition, provenance, rarity and which buyers happen to be bidding that day all change the price, and there is no standard unit like an ounce of gold.
