## What fails
A **government's promise to pay** its debts on time and in full. Default can mean missed payments, a forced exchange for new bonds worth less, or a long pause. It usually comes with recession, and often with currency losses and banking trouble.

## How often
More often than most people assume. Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's study of eight centuries of financial crises, *This Time Is Different*, found that most countries have defaulted at some point, many repeatedly. Debt that borrows in a **foreign currency** is far more exposed than debt in a currency the government issues itself, though a government can still default on its own currency's debt, as Russia did in 1998.

## The backstop
- **IMF lending,** usually with conditions such as spending cuts and reforms.
- **Restructuring:** a negotiated deal to cut or delay what's owed. Clauses in many modern bonds let a large majority of holders bind the rest.
- **Other governments,** bilaterally or through groups of creditors.

## Where the backstop runs out
- **Haircuts are real losses.** Restructurings usually cut what bondholders get back.
- **Holdouts and lawsuits** can drag on for years.
- **Domestic damage.** Banks that hold their own government's bonds can be dragged down with it. See [[bank-sovereign-loop]].

## Cases
| Country | When | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Russia | 17 August 1998 | Devalued the ruble, defaulted on domestic bonds and suspended foreign debt payments |
| Argentina | 2001 | Defaulted on its government debt, then the largest sovereign default on record |
| Greece | 2012 | Private bondholders took a 53.5% cut to face value, about €107 billion of debt relief |
| Sri Lanka | 12 April 2022 | Suspended most foreign debt payments: its first sovereign default |

## What it kills
That country's [[bonds]] first, then often its [[bank-deposits]] (through bank losses) and [[cash-and-fiat]] (through devaluation), and its [[shares]] in the recession that follows. [[human-capital]] suffers as jobs disappear.

> **Key rule:** "risk-free" means risk-free in the currency the government issues, and only while trust lasts. A government bond is still a promise, and history is full of broken ones.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why is government debt in a foreign currency riskier than debt in its own currency?

**A:** Because the government can't create foreign currency. To pay, it has to earn or borrow it, and in a crisis it may not be able to.

**Q:** In Greece's 2012 restructuring, private bondholders took a 53.5% cut to face value. Was that a default?

**A:** In substance yes: bondholders got back much less than they were promised, even though it was arranged as a negotiated exchange.
