## What it is
**Financial statements** are the reports a company publishes to show how it is doing. Listed companies must publish them regularly, checked by an independent **auditor**. There are three, and each answers a different question:

| Statement | Question it answers | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Income statement** | Did it make a profit? | A period, such as a year |
| **Balance sheet** | What does it own and owe? | One date |
| **Cash flow statement** | Where did the cash actually go? | A period |

## Why it exists
Shareholders and lenders can't see inside a company. Standard reports, prepared under accounting rules (IFRS in most countries; US GAAP in the United States), let outsiders compare companies and judge whether they are earning, borrowing and spending sensibly. They are the raw material of [[valuation]].

## How it works
**One fictional company, three views.**

**Income statement (the year):** revenue 500,000, minus the cost of the goods sold 300,000, gives **gross profit** of 200,000. Minus running costs of 120,000 gives **operating profit** of 80,000. Minus interest of 10,000 and tax of 14,000 leaves **net profit** of 56,000, a net margin of 11.2%.

**Balance sheet (year end):** the company owns assets of 400,000. It owes 250,000 (liabilities), so the owners' **equity** is 150,000. A balance sheet always balances:

> **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

The same split appears in [[claims-and-liabilities]]: creditors first, owners take what's left. Its short-term assets of 120,000 cover short-term debts of 80,000 by 1.5 times (the **current ratio**).

**Cash flow statement (the year):** the company made 56,000 of profit, but it spent 40,000 building up stock and customers owe it 30,000 more than last year. Adding back 10,000 of depreciation (a cost that isn't cash), its cash from operations is **−4,000**. Profitable on paper, short of cash in the bank.

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net profit | 56,000 |
| Add depreciation | +10,000 |
| More stock held | −40,000 |
| More owed by customers | −30,000 |
| **Cash from operations** | **−4,000** |

## What goes wrong
- **Profit isn't cash.** Companies fail when they run out of cash, not when they run out of profit. Growing stock and unpaid customer bills soak up money.
- **Judgment in the numbers.** Depreciation, stock values and when to count a sale all involve estimates, and estimates can be stretched.
- **Debt hidden in plain sight.** Leases, guarantees and pension promises can be large; read the notes, not only the headline figures.
- **Fraud.** Audits reduce it but can't rule it out.

> **Key rule:** read all three together. Profit that never turns into cash is a warning sign.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A company reports a record profit, but its cash from operations is negative. Name two possible reasons.

**A:** It may be building up stock faster than it sells it, or selling on credit to customers who haven't paid yet. Both count as sales in profit but tie up cash.
