## What it is
A **REIT** (real estate investment trust) is a company that owns, and usually runs, income-producing property, and whose shares trade on a stock exchange. Buying a REIT share means owning a small slice of a portfolio of buildings: offices, warehouses, flats, shopping centres, data centres.

The structure was created in the United States in 1960 so that ordinary savers could own commercial property. Many countries now have similar trusts.

## Why it exists
- **Small amounts.** You can own property for the price of one share.
- **Easy to sell.** Shares trade every day, unlike a building.
- **Spread.** One fund can own hundreds of buildings across cities and sectors.
- **Income.** In return for tax advantages, REITs must pay out most of their income. In the US, at least 90% of taxable income has to be distributed to shareholders.

## How it works
A REIT collects rent, pays its costs and interest, and passes most of the rest to shareholders as dividends. Its share price moves with the value of its buildings, with interest rates and with the stock market.

On the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]], a REIT share is not real estate in the title-deed sense. It's a [[claims-and-liabilities|claim]]: you own part of a company, held through a broker, so it sits with [[shares]] rather than with property you hold yourself.

| | Owning a flat | Owning REIT shares |
|---|---|---|
| **What you hold** | A title to one property | A share in a company owning many |
| **Selling** | Weeks to months, a few percent in costs | Seconds, small costs |
| **Work** | Tenants, repairs, insurance | None |
| **Price** | Valued now and then | Priced every trading day |
| **Leverage** | Your own mortgage | The company's borrowing |

## What goes wrong
- **Stock-market swings.** REIT prices can fall with shares in a crash, even if rents are still coming in.
- **Rate rises.** Higher interest rates raise a REIT's borrowing costs and make its dividends look less attractive next to bonds.
- **Sector shifts.** A REIT focused on one type of property shares its fate: offices after the move to remote work, shopping centres after the move online.
- **Company risk.** Poor management or too much debt can hurt shareholders even when property is doing well.

> **Key rule:** a REIT gives you property's income with a share's liquidity, and a share's swings.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A REIT's buildings are fully let and rents are unchanged, but its share price falls 20% in a market crash. Why can that happen?

**A:** Because the shares trade on the stock market. Their price reflects what buyers will pay that day, which moves with rates, fear and the wider market, not only with rents.
