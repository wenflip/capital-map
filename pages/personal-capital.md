## What it is
**Personal capital** is the wealth you carry inside you instead of in an account. It comes in two kinds:
- [[human-capital]] — your skills, knowledge, health and judgment: what lets you earn.
- [[social-capital]] — your reputation, network and the trust others place in you: what lets you be hired, backed, introduced and forgiven.

These are tier 1 on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]: embodied and mostly hidden. No register records them, no bank holds them, and nobody can confiscate them by decree.

## Why it exists
For most people, personal capital is the largest asset they will ever own, even though it never shows up on a statement. A young worker with no savings can still be wealthy in the economic sense: the value of decades of future earnings is large. Over a working life that wealth is slowly converted into financial capital, as wages are saved and invested.

It is also the **root store**. Money, property and shares can all be lost. Skills and relationships are the only stores that can rebuild the others from zero, which is why people who lose everything in a war or a crash often recover with what they know and who they know.

## How it works
- **It earns through work.** Human capital produces income only when you use it. Its "yield" is your wage or business profit.
- **It compounds.** Each new skill makes the next one easier to learn, and each person who trusts you can introduce you to another. See [[compounding]].
- **It depreciates.** Skills go out of date and networks drift apart unless they are maintained.
- **It can't be sold or split.** You can rent out your time, but you can't sell your skill or hand half of your reputation to someone else.

| | Human capital | Social capital |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | What you can do | Who trusts you |
| **Built by** | Study, practice, health | Delivering, over years |
| **Killer** | Death or incapacity | Betrayal or a reputation collapse |
| **Can heirs inherit it?** | No | Only partly, through introductions |

## What goes wrong
- **It dies with you.** The one thing the state and thieves can't take, illness and accidents can. That is what life and disability insurance are for: they turn part of an unmovable store into money.
- **Concentration.** If your job, your savings and your pension all depend on one employer or one industry, a single failure hits all of them at once. Your financial portfolio should usually lean *away* from the risks your career already carries. See [[diversification]].
- **Neglect.** A skill that the market stops paying for, or a network that hasn't been contacted in years, quietly loses value.

> **Key rule:** personal capital is your biggest asset and your least diversified one. Protect it with insurance, keep it current, and don't stack your financial bets on the same risk.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why is personal capital called the root store?

**A:** Because it is the only store that can rebuild the others from nothing. Lost money, property or shares can be earned back with skills and relationships; the reverse is much harder.

**Q:** You work for a large company and are offered the chance to put most of your savings into its shares. What is the risk?

**A:** Your income and your savings would depend on the same company. If it fails, you could lose your job and your savings at the same time.

## Going deeper
- **Human capital as an asset class.** Some financial planners treat future earnings as part of the portfolio. Someone with a safe, bond-like income can afford to hold more shares; someone whose income rises and falls with markets may want more bonds.
- **Hard to measure.** Economists estimate human capital as the present value of expected lifetime earnings, but the inputs (future pay, working years, the discount rate) are judgments, so the result is a rough range, not a number.
