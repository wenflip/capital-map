## What it is
**Rebalancing** means bringing a portfolio back to its target mix after prices have moved, by selling some of what has risen and buying some of what has fallen.

## Why it matters
Without it, a portfolio drifts towards whatever has done best, and becomes riskier than you chose. Rebalancing also builds in a discipline most people find hard: selling after a rise and buying after a fall.

## How it works
Fictional example: a 60/40 target on 100,000. Shares then rise 25% and bonds are unchanged.

| | Target | After the rise | Rebalanced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shares | 60,000 (60%) | 75,000 (65.2%) | 69,000 (60%) |
| Bonds | 40,000 (40%) | 40,000 (34.8%) | 46,000 (40%) |
| **Total** | 100,000 | 115,000 | 115,000 |

To get back to 60/40, sell **6,000** of shares and buy 6,000 of bonds.

**When to do it:**
- **By the calendar:** once or twice a year, whatever has happened.
- **By threshold:** whenever an asset drifts more than a set amount from target, such as 5 percentage points.
- **With new money:** direct new savings into whatever is below target, which avoids selling at all.

## What goes wrong
- **Costs and tax.** Every sale can bring trading costs and, outside tax-sheltered accounts, a tax bill. Rebalancing too often wastes money. See [[tax]].
- **Strong trends.** In a long rally, rebalancing keeps selling the winner, and in a long fall it keeps buying the loser. It controls risk; it doesn't promise extra return.
- **Losing nerve.** Buying the asset that has just crashed feels wrong, which is exactly when rebalancing matters most.

> **Key rule:** rebalance on a rule you set in advance, not on how the market feels.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A 50/50 portfolio of 200,000 sees shares fall to 80,000 while bonds stay at 100,000. What does rebalancing do?

**A:** The total is 180,000, so the target is 90,000 each. Sell 10,000 of bonds and buy 10,000 of shares.
