## What it is
The money a central bank issues. It comes in two forms:
- **Banknotes** — the physical cash the public can hold.
- **Reserves** — balances that commercial banks keep in accounts at the central bank. Access is limited, mainly to banks.

Both are the **central bank's liability**. Together they are often called the *monetary base*.

## Why it exists
Banks need a shared, final way to settle with each other. When your bank pays another bank, the final settlement is a transfer of reserves on the central bank's books. Everything above it — [[bank-deposits]], [[eurodollars]], [[stablecoins]] — is ultimately a promise to pay in central bank money.

## How it works
- **Creation.** The central bank creates reserves when it buys assets (usually government [[bonds]]) or lends to banks. It removes them when it sells assets or loans are repaid.
- **Policy rates.** The central bank sets the interest rate it pays on reserves and steers the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. That rate ripples out into other interest rates.
- **Balance sheet size.** Large asset purchases ("quantitative easing") expand reserves; letting assets run off ("quantitative tightening") shrinks them. This feeds directly into [[liquidity]].

## What goes wrong
- **Inflation** if money is created faster than the economy can absorb it. See [[inflation]].
- **Loss of trust** in the central bank or government, which pushes people towards assets lower in the [[hierarchy-of-money|hierarchy of money]], such as [[gold]], or towards foreign currencies.

> **Key rule:** central bank money is the base layer for almost everything you will hold, which is why central bank decisions move every market at once.
