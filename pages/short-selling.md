## What it is
**Short selling** is betting that a price will fall. The short seller borrows shares, sells them, and later buys them back to return to the lender. If the price has fallen, they keep the difference. If it has risen, they pay it.

**A short squeeze** is what can happen when a heavily shorted price rises: short sellers rush to buy back to limit their losses, and their buying pushes the price higher still.

## Why it exists
- **Price discovery.** Without short sellers, only optimists' views would reach the price.
- **Hedging.** A fund can protect against a fall in a market or sector it is exposed to.
- **Scrutiny.** Short sellers have strong reasons to dig for overvalued companies, accounting problems and fraud.

## How it works
**Fictional example.** You borrow 100 shares and sell them at 40, receiving 4,000.
- The price falls to 30. You buy them back for 3,000 and return them: a gain of **1,000**, before the fee for borrowing them.
- The price rises to 60 instead. Buying back costs 6,000: a loss of **2,000**.

**The losses are unlimited.** A share can fall at most to zero, so a buyer's loss is capped. A price can rise without limit, so a short seller's loss isn't.

**Short interest** is the number of shares sold short, often shown as a share of the stock that is freely traded. High short interest means many people are betting the same way, and all of them will one day have to buy.

**A real case: GameStop, January 2021.** The US regulator's staff later reported that short interest in GameStop reached about 123% of its freely traded shares that month. The price rose more than 1,600% between closes on 11 and 27 January, and touched $483 on 28 January. The same report found that buying by short sellers covering their positions was a small part of the buying: enthusiasm from other buyers, not the squeeze itself, sustained most of the rise. Some brokers restricted buying during the frenzy. See [[brokers]].

## What goes wrong
- **Being right too early.** A short seller can be right about a company and still be forced out by a rising price before it falls. See [[forced-sellers]].
- **Crowding.** When many shorts pile into the same stock, the exit is narrow.
- **Simple stories.** "It was a short squeeze" was the popular explanation for GameStop; the regulator's staff found a more mixed picture.

> **Key rule:** a short seller's loss has no ceiling. That's why crowded shorts can produce the most violent rises in the market.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You short 200 shares at 25. The price rises to 32 and you buy them back. What is your loss, before fees?

**A:** You sold for 5,000 and bought back for 6,400, a loss of **1,400**.
