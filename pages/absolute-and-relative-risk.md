## What it is
Relative risk describes how much larger or smaller one chance is compared with another. Absolute risk is the chance itself. A relative increase can sound large while the absolute chance remains very small.

## Why it matters
Headlines and summaries often use relative figures because they are larger. “Doubles the risk” can mean moving from 1 in a million to 2 in a million. The decision depends on the absolute chance and on the size of the harm, not on the relative multiplier alone.

## How it works
Convert the relative statement back to absolute numbers before judging it. If the baseline chance is already low, even a large relative change leaves a low absolute chance. If the baseline is high, a small relative change can matter. The same relative figure can describe very different situations depending on the starting point.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** a rare event has a baseline chance of 1 in 1,000,000. A factor doubles that chance. The new chance is 2 in 1,000,000, or 0.0002%. The relative increase is 100%. The absolute increase is 0.0001 percentage points. For most decisions the absolute numbers are the ones that matter.

## What goes wrong
- **Stopping at the relative figure.** “Doubles” or “halves” is reported without the baseline.
- **Treating all relative changes as equal.** Doubling a 0.001% chance is not the same as doubling a 10% chance.
- **Ignoring the size of the harm.** A tiny absolute chance of a catastrophic outcome can still matter; a large absolute chance of a trivial outcome may not.

> **Key rule:** turn every relative risk into absolute numbers before deciding whether it changes the choice.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A report says a choice “triples your risk”. The baseline chance is 0.01%. What is the new absolute chance?

**A:** 0.03% (0.01 × 3). The relative increase is large; the absolute chance remains small.

**Q:** Two studies both report a 50% relative reduction. One starts from a 20% baseline, the other from a 0.2% baseline. Are the absolute effects the same?

**A:** No. The first reduces the chance by 10 percentage points (20% × 0.5). The second reduces it by 0.1 percentage points (0.2% × 0.5).
