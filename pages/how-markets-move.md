## What it is
Prices don't move at random, and they don't move only on news. Underneath the daily noise are a few forces that repeat. People who work in markets learn them early. Most people never hear of them, and are left wondering why prices fell on good news, or why a sound asset was dumped at a terrible price.

This branch gathers those forces into three families:
- **Cycles:** the slow rhythms of the economy and of credit, and why calm periods plant the seeds of the next storm.
- **Expectations:** prices move on surprises against what was already expected, not on the news itself.
- **Flows:** who *has* to buy or sell at a given moment, whatever the asset is worth.

## Why it exists
The rest of the map explains what things are: a bond, a share, a deposit, a coin. This branch explains how their prices behave together, over time. Knowing it won't let anyone predict next week. It does explain much of what looks like madness, and it shows where the dangerous moments tend to be.

## How it works
**Cycles**
- [[business-cycle]] — expansion, peak, recession and recovery, and why markets turn before the economy does.
- [[credit-cycle]] — lending loosening and tightening in waves, usually ahead of the economy.
- [[stability-breeds-instability]] — why long calm periods build the borrowing that makes the next crash worse.

**Expectations**
- [[priced-in]] — prices move on the gap between what happens and what was expected.
- [[earnings-season]] — the reporting rhythm when those gaps show up company by company.
- [[fear-gauges]] — how markets measure their own nerves, through volatility and credit spreads.

**Flows**
- [[flows-and-fundamentals]] — the difference between what something is worth and who needs to trade it.
- [[forced-sellers]] — margin calls, redemptions and rules that make people sell at the worst price.
- [[short-selling]] — betting on a fall, and what happens when the bets are crowded.

Two close relatives live elsewhere on the map: the [[repo-market]], the overnight borrowing that funds much of the trading, under Markets & Plumbing; and [[asymmetry]], the shape of what you can win against what you can lose, under Portfolio & Risk.

## What goes wrong
- **Seeing patterns that aren't there.** Markets are noisy. A pattern found by searching enough charts will look convincing and then fail. The forces here are mechanisms with a reason behind them, not chart shapes.
- **Mistaking a mechanism for a timetable.** Knowing that cycles turn doesn't tell you when. Cycles vary in length, and the official dates arrive long after the turn.
- **Forgetting that everyone else knows too.** When a pattern becomes famous, people act on it early, and it weakens or changes. See [[priced-in]].

> **Key rule:** these forces explain how prices behave. They don't predict them. Use them to understand a move, and to avoid being the one forced to act at the worst moment, not as signals to trade on.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A company reports record profits and its share price falls. Name a reason this can happen.

**A:** The market expected even more. Prices move on the gap between results and expectations, so a record that falls short of what was priced in can still send the price down. See [[priced-in]].

**Q:** Why can a perfectly sound asset be sold at a very low price?

**A:** Because its owner had to sell, perhaps to meet a margin call or pay back fund investors, and couldn't wait for a better buyer. Forced selling ignores what an asset is worth. See [[forced-sellers]].

## Going deeper
- **Calendar rhythms** beyond earnings: central bank meeting days, the data calendar, options expiry dates and index rebalancing all bring predictable bursts of trading.
- **Behaviour:** herding, fear of missing out and loss aversion explain why the same mistakes repeat across generations. See [[common-mistakes]].
- **Sector rotation:** different kinds of business tend to lead at different stages of the [[business-cycle]], a pattern that is loose and often broken.
