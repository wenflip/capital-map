## What it is
"Liquidity" has two meanings in finance:
1. **Market liquidity:** how easily you can buy or sell an asset without moving its price.
2. **System liquidity:** how much money is available in the financial system to buy assets.

This page is about the second. It is driven mainly by central banks and governments.

## Why it matters
When more money is chasing the same assets, prices tend to rise, especially for riskier assets such as growth shares and crypto. When money drains out of the system, the riskiest assets usually fall first and hardest. This is a big part of why crypto has often moved with high-growth tech shares. See [[market-regimes]].

## How it is measured
A common market proxy for US dollar liquidity, often called **net liquidity**:

| Part | What it is | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Central bank balance sheet | Assets bought with newly created reserves | Bigger = more money in the system |
| Treasury General Account (TGA) | The government's cash account at the central bank | Money parked here is out of the system |
| Reverse repo facility | Cash that money market funds and other eligible firms park at the central bank overnight | Money parked here is out of the system |

**Net liquidity ≈ balance sheet − TGA − reverse repo**

This is a rule of thumb used by market analysts, not an official measure.

## What moves it
- Asset purchases and run-offs by the central bank. See [[central-bank-money]].
- Government tax receipts and spending, which move cash in and out of the TGA.
- Money funds shifting between the reverse repo facility and other short-term assets such as [[t-bills]]. See [[repo-market]].

> **Key rule:** liquidity explains a lot of what moves across many assets at once, but not why any single asset is worth what it is.

## Check yourself

**Q:** The government collects a large tax payment and its TGA balance rises. What happens to net liquidity?

**A:** It falls. Money moves out of bank deposits into the government's account at the central bank, where it is out of the system.
