## What it is
A **share** (or **stock**) is one unit of ownership in a company. Owning shares in a listed company gives you:
- a claim on part of its profits, paid as **dividends** if the board decides to pay them;
- a **vote** on major decisions such as electing the board;
- the **residual claim**: what's left if the company is sold or wound up, after all creditors are paid.

## Why it exists
Selling shares lets a company raise money it never has to repay. A listing on a stock exchange lets owners sell to someone else at any time, which makes people far more willing to invest in the first place.

## How it works
- **Going public.** A company first sells shares to the public in an **initial public offering** (IPO). After that, shares change hands between investors on [[exchanges]]; the company isn't involved.
- **Returns.** You earn from dividends and from changes in the share price. Some companies pay no dividend and reinvest everything; others buy back their own shares.
- **Holding.** Today most shares are held electronically through a broker, often in the broker's name for your benefit (a **nominee** account). See [[brokers]] and [[custody]].

## What goes wrong
- **The company fails.** Shares can go to zero, because shareholders are paid last. See [[claims-and-liabilities]].
- **The market falls.** Even strong companies' shares drop in a crash. The S&P 500 lost about 57% from October 2007 to March 2009.
- **The broker fails.** Your shares should be held separately from the broker's own money, and many countries have a protection scheme. In the US, SIPC covers up to $500,000 per customer (including up to $250,000 of cash) if a broker fails and customer assets are missing. It does not cover a fall in share prices. This is the counterparty killer on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]].
- **Dilution.** If a company issues new shares, each existing share owns a smaller slice.

> **Key rule:** a share is a residual claim held through an intermediary. Diversify across companies, and know how your broker holds your shares.

## How it has failed
- **Wiped out, 1917 and 1949.** After the Russian revolution and the Communist victory in China, stock markets closed and shareholders lost everything. Long-run studies of share returns mostly measure markets that survived.
- **The 1929 crash.** The Dow Jones Industrial Average lost about 89% between 1929 and 1932, and didn't regain its 1929 peak until 1954.
- **Japan, 1989–2024.** The Nikkei 225 closed at 38,915.87 on 29 December 1989. It didn't close higher until 22 February 2024, 34 years later.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Your broker goes bust, and separately your shares have fallen 30% in a market slump. Which of these would SIPC help with in the US?

**A:** Only the broker failure, and only if customer assets are missing, up to the limits. SIPC doesn't protect against prices falling.
