## What it is
**Monetary policy** is how a central bank steers the price and amount of money in an economy. Its main tool is one short-term interest rate, the **policy rate**. Its second tool is the size of its own balance sheet: how many bonds it holds, paid for with [[central-bank-money|reserves]] it creates.

Most central banks are given a goal by law. The most common is **stable prices**, usually written as an inflation target. New Zealand was the first to adopt a formal target in its modern form, under a law that came into force in February 1990. Some central banks have a second goal too: the US central bank, for example, is also asked to aim for maximum employment, and it named 2% inflation as its long-run goal in January 2012.

## Why it exists
Money only works if people can trust what it will buy next year. Too much spending chasing too few goods pushes prices up; too little drags the economy down and puts people out of work. Monetary policy leans against both: it makes borrowing dearer when the economy runs too hot, and cheaper when it runs too cold. See [[inflation]].

It is different from the central bank's other job, lending in a panic. That is about keeping banks alive for a few days, not about steering the economy. See [[lender-of-last-resort]].

## How it works
**Setting the rate.** The central bank pays interest on the reserves banks hold with it, and lends to banks against good collateral. No bank will lend reserves overnight for much less than the central bank pays, or borrow for much more than it charges, so the overnight rate between banks stays close to the policy rate.

**How the rate reaches you.** A change in the overnight rate spreads out through several channels:
- **Borrowing costs.** Rates on floating-rate loans, new mortgages and [[t-bills]] move with it. Say you have a floating-rate loan of 200,000 and the policy rate rises by 2 percentage points (fictional figures): your interest bill rises by 200,000 × 2% = **4,000** a year.
- **Asset prices.** Higher rates make future income worth less now, so [[bonds]], shares and property tend to fall. See [[time-value-of-money]].
- **The currency.** Higher rates attract money from abroad, which tends to lift the currency. See [[exchange-rates]].
- **Expectations.** Much of the effect comes from what people think the central bank will do next. Central banks steer this with **forward guidance**: saying in advance how they expect to act.

The effect takes time. Many loans are fixed for years, and firms plan investment long in advance, so a rate change can take a year or more to reach prices fully.

**Real and nominal rates.** What matters to a borrower or saver is the rate after inflation. Say the policy rate is 5% and inflation is 3% (fictional figures). The rough real rate is 5% − 3% = 2%; exactly, 1.05 ÷ 1.03 = 1.019, a real rate of **1.9%**. A positive real rate holds spending back; a negative one encourages it.

**Below zero.** When rates reach zero, a central bank has less room to cut. Some went below it: the European Central Bank set a deposit rate of −0.10% from 11 June 2014, and the Bank of Japan ended its negative rate on 19 March 2024, its first rise in seventeen years. Negative rates charge banks for holding reserves, to push them to lend instead.

**The balance sheet: QE and QT.** When rate cuts aren't enough, a central bank can buy government bonds on a large scale with new reserves. This **quantitative easing** (QE) pushes long-term yields down and floods the system with [[liquidity]]. **Quantitative tightening** (QT) is the reverse: the bonds mature and aren't replaced, and reserves shrink. The flow *QE and QT* follows each step. See [[yield-curve]].

## What goes wrong
- **Too loose for too long.** Inflation builds and becomes hard to stop once people expect it. In the US, inflation peaked at 11.6% in March 1980; after a change of approach announced in October 1979, the federal funds rate reached a record 20% in late 1980. Inflation came down, after two recessions in the early 1980s.
- **Too tight, or too late.** Rates that rise too far or stay high too long can push an economy into recession, and the long lags make it easy to overshoot.
- **Running out of room.** Near zero, cuts lose their force, and central banks turn to tools whose side effects are less well understood.
- **Serving the government instead.** When a government's debt is large, there is pressure to hold rates down to keep its interest bill low, even when inflation is rising. See [[financial-repression]] and, at the far end, [[currency-failure]].

> **Key rule:** the policy rate is the price of the base layer of money. When it moves, every other price that depends on borrowing, discounting or a currency moves after it.

## Check yourself

**Q:** The policy rate is 1% and inflation is 4%. What is the real rate, roughly and exactly, and what does it encourage?

**A:** Roughly 1% − 4% = −3%. Exactly, 1.01 ÷ 1.04 = 0.971, a real rate of **−2.9%**. A negative real rate rewards borrowing and spending, and punishes holding cash.

**Q:** Why can a rate rise take a year or more to bring inflation down?

**A:** Because many loans are fixed for years, wages and prices are reset only from time to time, and firms plan spending ahead. The new rate reaches the economy only as old contracts end and new decisions are made.

## Going deeper
- **The Taylor rule.** In 1993 John Taylor described a simple rule that tracked US policy well: start from a neutral real rate, add inflation, then add half the gap between inflation and its target and half the gap between output and its potential. With a neutral real rate of 2%, inflation of 3%, a 2% target and output 1% above potential (fictional figures): 2% + 3% + 0.5 × (3% − 2%) + 0.5 × 1% = **6%**. It is a guide central banks compare themselves to, not a law they follow.
- **Floor and corridor systems.** When reserves are scarce, the central bank steers the overnight rate by adding or draining small amounts of them. Since QE made reserves plentiful, many central banks run a **floor** system instead, where the rate paid on reserves sets the overnight rate directly.
- **Central bank losses.** Paying interest on a large pile of reserves while holding bonds bought at low yields can leave a central bank with losses for years. Because it issues its own currency, it can keep operating; see [[currency-failure]].
