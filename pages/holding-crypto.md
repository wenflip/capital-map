## What it is
There are two ways to hold a digital asset, and they are different assets:
- [[self-custody]] — you hold the keys. A bearer asset: Tier 2 on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]].
- [[custodial-crypto]] — an exchange, broker or fund holds the keys. A claim on that firm: Tier 3.

## Why it matters
The coin and its price are the same either way; the risk isn't. Most people who have lost crypto lost it through how it was held, not because the coin itself failed.

## How it works
| | Self-custody | Custodial |
|---|---|---|
| Who controls the keys | You | The firm |
| What you own | The coins | A claim on the firm |
| Main killer | Losing or leaking your seed phrase | The firm failing, freezing or being hacked |
| Recourse | None | Courts, regulators, insurance (varies) |
| Visible to the state | Not necessarily | Yes, through the firm |
| Inheritance | Heirs need the keys | Heirs go through the firm |

Many holders use both: a custodial account for buying and selling, and self-custody for long-term holdings.

## What goes wrong
- Treating a custodial balance as if it were coins in your own wallet.
- Moving to self-custody without a plan for backups and inheritance.

> **Key rule:** decide which killer you would rather face, then manage that one deliberately.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You can see 1 bitcoin in your exchange account. Do you own a bitcoin?

**A:** You own a claim on the exchange for 1 bitcoin. If the exchange fails, you are a creditor in its bankruptcy, not the owner of a specific coin.
