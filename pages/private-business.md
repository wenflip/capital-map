## What it is
A **private business** is a company whose shares aren't listed on a stock exchange. You might own one by:
- **running it yourself:** a shop, a trade, a consultancy;
- **owning a family business**, or a stake in a friend's;
- **investing early** in a start-up as an angel investor;
- **investing through a fund:** private equity buys established companies, venture capital backs young ones.

## Why people own it
- **The highest upside.** A successful business can grow far beyond what you put in.
- **Control.** You decide how it's run, at least if you own most of it.
- **Turning personal capital into an asset.** A business is often the owner's skills and relationships packaged into something that can be sold. See [[personal-capital]].

## How it works
**Valuing it.** There is no daily price. Buyers usually pay a **multiple of profits**, then knock off a discount because the stake is hard to sell, and often another if it depends heavily on one person. Every sale is a negotiation.

**Private equity funds** typically lock investors' money up for around ten years, buy companies, often with a lot of borrowed money, try to improve them, and sell. Fees are high, commonly a yearly management fee plus a share of the profits.

| | Listed shares | Private business |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Every trading day | At sale or appraisal |
| **Selling** | Seconds | Months, if a buyer exists |
| **Information** | Public, audited | What the owners choose to share |
| **Spread** | Easy across hundreds | Often one company |

## What goes wrong
- **Concentration and management**, the killer on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]. One bad decision, one lost customer, or one key person leaving can end it.
- **Most new businesses don't last.** Of US business locations opened in March 2013, 79.6% were still operating a year later, 50.6% after five years and 34.7% after ten.
- **No exit.** Owners often find there is no buyer at the price they hoped for, or that the business is worth little without them.
- **Everything in one place.** An owner's income, savings and often their home (as security for a loan) can all depend on the same business.

> **Key rule:** a private business can be your best asset and your least diversified one. Build savings outside it, and make it able to run without you.

## How it has failed
- **Most don't last.** Of US business locations opened in March 2013, only 34.7% were still operating ten years later.
- **A big customer fails.** When the UK construction company Carillion went into liquidation in January 2018, around 30,000 suppliers were owed about £1 billion. Small firms that depended on it lost their main customer and their unpaid invoices together.
- **The owner leaves.** Many small businesses can't be sold because much of their value walks out of the door with the owner.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why do buyers often pay less for a business that depends on its owner?

**A:** Because much of its value may walk out of the door when the owner leaves. The buyer is paying for profits that might not continue without them.
