## What fails
**Ownership itself.** A state can take an asset by law: requiring citizens to hand over gold, nationalising a company or an industry, or freezing another country's or person's assets. Unlike theft, it's done in the open, under rules the owner can't override.

## The backstop
- **Courts and constitutions**, where property rights are protected and judges are independent.
- **Compensation**, sometimes paid, often less than the owner thinks the asset was worth.
- **International agreements** that protect foreign investors, and arbitration to enforce them.

## Where the backstop runs out
- **The state writes the rules.** Courts can only enforce the law as it stands, and laws can change.
- **Compensation is negotiated,** and may take years.
- **Assets held abroad** are under the host country's law. Holding money elsewhere protects against your own government, but exposes you to someone else's.
- **Visibility matters.** The more legible an asset, the easier it is to reach. That's the vertical axis of the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]].

## Cases
- **US gold, 1933.** An executive order required citizens to hand in most gold coins, bullion and certificates at a fixed price. The dollar was then devalued against gold. See [[gold]].
- **Mexico, 18 March 1938.** Mexico expropriated foreign oil companies. Compensation was settled later: about $29 million to American firms in 1942, and $130 million to British interests in 1947.
- **Russia's reserves, 2022.** After the invasion of Ukraine, Western countries froze about $300 billion of the Russian central bank's reserves, roughly half of its foreign currency and gold. Even a central bank's reserves held abroad are subject to the host countries' law.

## What it kills
Assets the state can see and reach: [[real-estate]], [[bank-deposits]], [[shares]] in strategic companies, and [[gold]] where its ownership is recorded or known. [[human-capital]] and [[social-capital]] are the hardest to confiscate, which is why the Cartography treats them as the root store.

> **Key rule:** every asset is held under someone's law. Know whose, and whether that law protects owners like you.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why can holding assets abroad both reduce and add confiscation risk?

**A:** It moves them out of reach of your own government, but puts them under the law of the country that holds them, as Russia's frozen reserves showed in 2022.

**Q:** How is confiscation different from theft?

**A:** It's done openly by a state under its own laws, so courts may be unable to reverse it, and any compensation is set by the same state.
