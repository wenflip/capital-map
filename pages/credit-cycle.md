## What it is
The **credit cycle** is the rise and fall in how easy it is to borrow. In some years lenders compete to lend, ask for little security and charge thin margins over safe rates. In others they pull back, ask for more, and charge more, or don't lend at all.

Because most spending on homes, businesses and investments is borrowed, the credit cycle drives much of the [[business-cycle]], and usually turns first.

## Why it matters
New loans create new money and new spending. See [[money-creation]]. When lending grows, prices of the things bought with credit (houses, companies, shares) tend to rise. When it shrinks, the same assets lose the buyers they relied on, just as defaults rise.

## How it works
**The loosening loop.** Rising prices make the security behind loans look safe. Lenders relax their standards, more people borrow, and the extra buying pushes prices up further.

**The tightening loop.** Defaults rise, so lenders tighten. Less credit means less spending, which brings more defaults. The same loop that inflated prices now deflates them.

**Fictional example: how much room a borrower has.** A home costs 300,000.
- With a 95% loan, the borrower owes 285,000. If the price falls 10% to 270,000, the loan is 15,000 *more* than the home is worth.
- With an 80% loan, the borrower owes 240,000. After the same fall there is still 30,000 of the borrower's own money in the home.

Late in a credit boom, the 95% loans are the common ones, so a modest fall leaves many borrowers owing more than they own.

**How people watch it.**
- **Lending surveys.** In the US, the central bank asks senior loan officers every quarter whether they have tightened or eased their standards, and whether demand for loans is rising or falling.
- **Credit spreads,** the extra interest risky borrowers pay over safe ones, which widen as lenders grow nervous. See [[credit-risk]] and [[fear-gauges]].
- **Default rates,** which rise late, after the damage is done.

## What goes wrong
- **Easy credit hides weak borrowers.** When anyone can refinance, no one looks like a bad risk.
- **Tightening hits everyone at once.** Borrowers who planned to roll their debts over find the door shut at the same moment.
- **Lending against rising prices.** Loans secured on assets whose price was lifted by the loans themselves are safe only while the boom lasts.

> **Key rule:** the best time to borrow is the most dangerous time to be over-borrowed. Ask what happens to your loan if lending tightens just when you need to refinance.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A home worth 200,000 is bought with a 90% loan. Its price then falls 15%. How much more is owed than the home is worth?

**A:** The loan is 180,000 and the home is now worth 170,000, so the borrower owes **10,000** more than the home is worth.
