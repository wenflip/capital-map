## What it is
A **portfolio** is everything you hold, looked at together. Portfolio thinking asks a different question from picking investments: not "is this asset good?" but "what does this asset do to the whole?"

The main idea of this branch is the one at the centre of the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]: **the enemy is correlation, not risk.** Every store of wealth has something that can kill it. A portfolio survives when no single killer can hit everything at once.

## Why it exists
Nobody knows which asset will do best next year. Putting everything into the best guess means one wrong guess can be fatal. Combining assets that fail for different reasons gives a steadier result than any one of them alone, often with little loss of return. This is the closest thing finance has to a free lunch.

## How it works
- [[diversification]] — spreading across assets that don't fail together, and the risk that can't be spread away.
- [[correlation]] — the number that measures how closely two assets move, and why it rises in a crisis.
- [[asset-allocation]] — the mix across shares, bonds, cash and real assets, which drives most of a portfolio's behaviour.
- [[position-sizing]] — how much to put in any one holding so that being wrong is survivable.
- [[rebalancing]] — bringing the mix back to target as prices move.
- [[market-regimes]] — why the same mix behaves very differently in different conditions.

| Question | Page | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|
| What should I own? | Asset allocation | Match the mix to when you need the money |
| How much of each? | Position sizing | No single loss you can't recover from |
| Do they fail together? | Correlation | Check what happens in a crisis, not on average |
| What now prices have moved? | Rebalancing | Sell some of what rose, buy some of what fell |

## What goes wrong
- **False diversification.** Ten technology funds are one bet. So are shares, property and a job that all depend on the same local economy.
- **Correlations jump in a crisis.** Assets that moved independently in calm years can all fall together when everyone sells at once. In 2022, US shares fell about 18% and US bonds about 13% in the same year, a rare tandem fall.
- **Concentration by accident.** A winning holding grows until it dominates, unless you rebalance.
- **Too much leverage.** Borrowing turns a survivable fall into a forced sale. See [[leverage]].

> **Key rule:** judge every holding by what it does to the whole, and ask what single event could hit everything you own.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You own shares in five different banks in one country. Are you diversified?

**A:** Only a little. They are different companies but share the same risks: the same economy, interest rates and regulator. A banking crisis would likely hit all five together.

**Q:** Why does a portfolio of two volatile assets sometimes swing less than either asset on its own?

**A:** Because when the assets don't move perfectly together, one's falls are partly offset by the other's rises. The lower their correlation, the more the swings cancel out.

## Going deeper
- **Modern portfolio theory.** Harry Markowitz showed in 1952 that investors should choose portfolios, not single assets, by the balance of expected return and risk of the whole. For any level of risk there is a mix with the highest expected return; the set of those mixes is called the **efficient frontier**.
- **Its limits.** The theory needs estimates of returns, volatilities and correlations, and those estimates are unstable. Small changes in the inputs can produce very different "optimal" portfolios, which is why many investors use simple, robust mixes instead.
