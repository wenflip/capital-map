## What it is
A bank deposit is a promise by your bank. If the bank can't keep it, a **chain of backstops** is supposed to step in, roughly in this order:

| Order | Link | What it offers | Page |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The bank itself** | Its own capital and liquid assets | [[bank-capital-and-liquidity]] |
| 2 | **The central bank** | Emergency lending while the bank is only short of cash | [[lender-of-last-resort]] |
| 3 | **The bank's investors** | Losses imposed on shareholders, bondholders and large depositors | [[bail-in]] |
| 4 | **Deposit insurance** | A fund that repays deposits up to a limit | [[deposit-insurance]] |
| 5 | **The government** | Public money to rescue a bank, or to back the insurance fund | [[bank-sovereign-loop]] |

## Why it matters
Whether you lose money in a bank failure depends on **which link breaks**, and whether the next link is strong enough to catch it. A small bank failing in a large, solvent country is caught early in the chain. A banking system bigger than its own government can bring the whole chain down.

## How it works
Each link is only as good as its size relative to the problem:
- A bank with thick capital absorbs losses itself.
- A central bank can lend unlimited amounts of its own currency to banks that are solvent but short of cash; it shouldn't lend to banks whose losses exceed their capital.
- Where the law says so, losses then go to the bank's investors and large depositors before taxpayers.
- Deposit insurance repays small depositors; it handles one or two failures, not a whole system.
- The government can borrow to rescue banks — if it can borrow at all.

**Real cases:**
| Case | What happened | Which link held |
|---|---|---|
| United States, 2023 | Silicon Valley Bank failed after losses on bonds and a fast run | The government and insurance fund protected **all** deposits, including those above the limit |
| Iceland, 2008 | Banks with assets over 11 times the country's annual output collapsed | Domestic deposits were guaranteed; depositors in foreign branches lost access, and a long dispute followed |
| Cyprus, 2013 | Two large banks failed while the government couldn't borrow | Insured deposits were protected; large deposits took heavy losses |
| Switzerland, 2023 | Credit Suisse was taken over by UBS | Depositors were protected; about 16 billion Swiss francs of the bank's riskiest bonds were written to zero |
| Lebanon, from 2019 | Banks restricted withdrawals as the state's finances collapsed | No link held: deposits were frozen for years |

## What goes wrong
- **The chain is only as strong as its last link.** Insurance funds hold a small fraction of the deposits they cover, so in a systemic crisis the government behind them is what really matters.
- **The links are tied together.** Banks often hold large amounts of their own government's debt, so a weak government weakens its banks and the other way round.
- **Rescues aren't guaranteed.** Whether uninsured depositors are protected is decided at the time, case by case.

> **Key rule:** a deposit is only as safe as the strongest link still standing behind it. Ask how big each link is compared with what it might have to cover.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why could the United States protect every depositor at a failed bank in 2023, when Iceland couldn't do the same for foreign depositors in 2008?

**A:** Because of relative size. The failed US banks were small compared with the US government's ability to borrow; Iceland's banks were many times larger than its whole economy.

**Q:** Deposit insurance covers your deposit. Why does the government's own health still matter?

**A:** Because insurance funds hold only a small fraction of the deposits they cover. In a systemic crisis the fund needs the government behind it, and if the government can't borrow, the promise weakens.
