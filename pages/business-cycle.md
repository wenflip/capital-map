## What it is
The **business cycle** is the economy's habit of growing for a while, stalling, shrinking, and then growing again. It isn't regular like a clock: some expansions run for a decade, some recessions last a couple of months. But the stages repeat:

| Stage | What happens in the economy | What tends to happen in markets |
|---|---|---|
| **Expansion** | Hiring, rising spending and profits, easy borrowing | Shares and riskier debt tend to do well |
| **Late cycle** | Spare capacity runs out, prices and wages rise, central banks raise rates | Gains narrow; bonds may fall as rates rise |
| **Recession** | Spending falls, job losses, more defaults | Shares and riskier debt tend to fall; central banks cut rates |
| **Recovery** | Spending picks up from a low base | Markets often rise strongly, usually before the data improves |

"Tend to" matters: these are patterns, not rules, and every cycle has its own shape.

## Why it matters
Jobs, profits, defaults, interest rates and asset prices all move with the cycle. A large part of what happens in markets in any year traces back to where the economy is in it, and to where investors think it is going next.

## How it works
**Markets look ahead.** Prices reflect what investors expect, not what has already happened. So share markets often fall before a recession is announced, and often bottom while the news is still bad. See [[priced-in]].

**Dating a recession.** A common rule of thumb calls two quarters in a row of falling output a recession. Official dating bodies use a wider set of measures and announce their dates long afterwards. In the US, the National Bureau of Economic Research dated the financial-crisis recession from **December 2007 to June 2009**, and the pandemic recession from **February to April 2020**.

**Credit and policy drive the turns.** Easy borrowing feeds expansions and tight borrowing ends them. See [[credit-cycle]] and [[monetary-policy]].

## What goes wrong
- **Calling it in real time.** The official verdict comes months late. By then markets have usually moved.
- **"This time is different."** Long expansions convince people that recessions are over. See [[stability-breeds-instability]].
- **Expecting a timetable.** Cycles differ in length and depth; averages from the past say little about the next one.

> **Key rule:** the cycle explains why the same asset can be a good or bad holding in different years. Build for every stage, rather than betting on the next turn.

## Check yourself

**Q:** The US recession dated by the NBER ran from December 2007 to June 2009. How long did it last?

**A:** 18 months, counting from the peak month to the trough month.

**Q:** Why can share prices rise while the economy is still in recession?

**A:** Because prices are set by expectations of future profits. When investors start to expect a recovery, they buy before the data shows one, so markets often turn up before the economy does.
