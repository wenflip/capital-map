## What it is
**Deposit insurance** is a scheme that repays depositors, up to a limit per person per bank, if their bank fails. Most countries have one, usually run by a public agency and funded by fees charged to banks.

## Why it exists
If depositors fear losing their money, they run at the first rumour, and even healthy banks can fail. Insurance removes the reason for small depositors to run.

## How it works
- **A limit.** Deposits up to a set amount per depositor per bank are covered; anything above is not.
- **A fund.** Banks pay regular fees into a fund that pays out when a bank fails.
- **The fund is small compared with what it covers.** It typically holds only a small percentage of insured deposits. That is enough for occasional failures, not for many large banks at once.
- **A government backstop.** In most systems the government stands behind the fund, which is what makes the promise credible in a crisis.

## What goes wrong
- **Deposits above the limit** are exposed. Businesses and wealthy savers often hold much more than the limit in one bank.
- **Systemic crises** can exceed the fund many times over, so the guarantee is only as good as the government behind it.
- **Moral hazard.** Insured depositors have little reason to watch how their bank takes risks, and banks may take more because of it.
- **Discretion.** Governments sometimes protect deposits above the limit, as the United States did in 2023, but that is decided case by case, not promised.

> **Key rule:** know the limit and stay under it per bank — and remember that the fund behind it depends on the government behind that.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why do economists describe a deposit insurance fund as built for single failures, not for crises?

**A:** Because it holds only a small fraction of the deposits it covers. One bank failing is affordable; many large ones failing together would empty it, leaving the government to make good the promise.
