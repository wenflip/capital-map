## What it is
**Equities** are ownership stakes in businesses. A bondholder lends to a company and is promised fixed payments; a shareholder owns part of it and gets whatever is left after everyone else has been paid. That leftover is called the **residual claim**: unlimited on the way up, and possibly nothing on the way down.

## Why it exists
Companies need money that doesn't have to be paid back on a set date. Selling ownership gives them that: shareholders share in the profits instead of being owed interest. Savers, in return, get a way to own a part of the economy's growth, not just lend to it.

Over long periods, shares have usually returned more than bonds or cash. That extra return is the reward for taking the residual claim: being paid last, and living with large falls along the way.

|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Set by trading every day | Estimated now and then |

## What goes wrong
- **Company failure.** A shareholder is last in line, so a bankrupt company's shares are often worth nothing. See [[claims-and-liabilities]].
- **Market crashes.** The S&P 500 fell about 57% between its October 2007 peak and its March 2009 low. Diversification across companies doesn't help when the whole market falls together. See [[drawdown]].
- **Paying too much.** A good company can be a poor investment if the price already assumes years of perfect growth. See [[valuation]].
- **Holding through someone else.** Listed shares are held through a broker, the killer on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]: you own a ledger entry, not a certificate in a drawer.

> **Key rule:** shares pay for being last in line. Hold them for long periods, across many companies, and never with money you will need during a crash.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A company fails. Its bondholders recover 40% of what they are owed. What do its shareholders usually get?

**A:** Usually nothing. Shareholders are paid only after every creditor, including bondholders, has been paid in full.

**Q:** Why have shares usually returned more than bonds over long periods?

**A:** Because shareholders take more risk: they are paid last, have no promised payments, and can suffer large falls. The higher average return is what persuades people to take that risk.

## Going deeper
- **The equity risk premium.** The extra return investors expect from shares over safe government bonds. It can't be observed directly; estimates differ depending on the period and method, and it varies over time.
- **Share classes.** Some companies issue shares with different voting rights, so founders can keep control while selling most of the economic ownership.
