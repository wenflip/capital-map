## What it is
**Position sizing** is deciding how much to put into any single holding or trade. Its goal is simple: being wrong about any one thing should never be fatal.

## Why it matters
Losses and gains aren't symmetrical. After a fall, you need a bigger percentage gain just to get back to where you started:

| Loss | Gain needed to recover |
|---|---|
| −10% | +11.1% |
| −20% | +25.0% |
| −50% | +100.0% |
| −90% | +900.0% |

Small losses are easy to recover from; large ones may never be. Sizing keeps each mistake in the top rows of that table. See [[drawdown]].

## How it works
**Cap any single holding.** Many investors limit any one company to a small share of the portfolio, so its failure is a setback, not a disaster.

**Size by what you can lose, not by how sure you feel.** A common trading rule is to risk a fixed fraction of the portfolio on each idea. Fictional example: a 50,000 portfolio, risking 1% per trade, with a plan to sell if the price falls 10%:
- Amount at risk: 1% of 50,000 = **500**.
- Position size: 500 ÷ 10% = **5,000**.

If the price falls to the exit point, the loss is 500, and the portfolio can absorb many such mistakes.

**Volatile holdings get smaller positions.** Something that swings three times as much as another should usually take a smaller share, so each contributes a similar amount of risk.

## What goes wrong
- **Conviction sizing.** The holdings people feel surest about often become the biggest, which is how one mistake becomes a catastrophe.
- **Winners that grow unchecked.** A position that doubles twice can quietly become most of the portfolio.
- **Leverage.** Borrowing to buy multiplies the size of every loss. See [[leverage]].
- **Hidden overlap.** Several holdings that share one killer are one position.

> **Key rule:** size every position so that being completely wrong about it is survivable.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A holding falls 50%. What gain does it need to get back to where it started?

**A:** 100%. Half of the original is left, so it has to double to recover.
