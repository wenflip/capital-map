## What it is
A **futures contract** is an agreement to buy or sell a set amount of something on a set future date, at a price agreed today. Futures trade on exchanges in standard sizes: a set number of barrels of oil, tonnes of wheat, or units of a share index.

## Why it exists
- **Producers** sell ahead to lock in a price for what they will produce.
- **Users** buy ahead to lock in what they will pay.
- **Investors and traders** use futures to gain or reduce exposure quickly and cheaply.

## How it works
**Margin, not the full price.** You don't pay for the goods up front. Both sides put down a deposit called **margin**, held by the exchange's **clearing house**, which stands between buyer and seller.

**Marked to market daily.** Every day, gains are paid into your account and losses taken out. If your account falls below a minimum, you must top it up (a **margin call**) or the position is closed.

**Fictional example:** a contract for 1,000 barrels of oil at 70, so a value of 70,000. Margin is 10%: **7,000**.
- Oil rises to 77: the position gains 7,000, **+100%** on the margin.
- Oil falls to 63: it loses 7,000, **−100%**, and the whole deposit is gone.

A 10% price move doubled or wiped out the deposit. See [[leverage]].

**Most futures never end in delivery.** Traders close or roll their positions before expiry. See [[commodities]] for how rolling works.

## What goes wrong
- **Losses beyond the deposit.** A large move can wipe out the margin and leave you owing more.
- **Margin calls at the worst time,** forcing sales in a falling market.
- **Delivery and storage.** Near expiry, holders who can't take delivery must sell. In April 2020 that pushed one US oil contract below zero.

> **Key rule:** a futures position is the whole contract value, not the deposit. Size it on the contract value.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You hold a futures contract worth 50,000 with 5,000 of margin. The price falls 4%. How much of your margin is lost?

**A:** 2,000, which is 40% of the margin. A 4% fall on 50,000 is 2,000.
