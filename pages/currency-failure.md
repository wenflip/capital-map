## What fails
The **currency itself**. People stop trusting it to hold value, so they spend it as fast as possible, prices race upward, and savings in that currency are wiped out. In the worst cases the currency is abandoned for another.

## A central bank can't go bust, but its money can
A central bank that issues its own currency can always pay its own bills by creating more of it. Central banks have at times run for years with **negative equity**, owing more than they own on paper, and kept operating; the Bank for International Settlements notes this can be fully consistent with keeping trust in money. So a currency doesn't fail like a company, through bankruptcy. It fails when people **stop accepting it**, usually because the government has financed itself by printing far beyond what the economy can absorb.

## The backstop
For individuals, the backstops are holdings outside that currency: other currencies, [[real-assets]], [[gold]], and businesses that earn in stronger money. Governments sometimes end a hyperinflation by tying their currency to another, introducing a new currency, or adopting another country's.

## Where the backstop runs out
- **Your income and debts are in the failing currency,** so moving savings doesn't protect your job or your rent.
- **Controls often follow,** blocking access to foreign money. See [[capital-controls]].
- **Redenominations reset prices, not losses.** Removing zeros makes the numbers smaller; it doesn't return lost value.

## Cases
- **Germany, 1923.** By November 1923 one US dollar was worth about 4.2 trillion marks.
- **Zimbabwe, 2008.** Steve Hanke estimated monthly inflation in November 2008 at 79.6 billion percent. The country stopped using its own currency in 2009.
- **Venezuela, 2008–21.** The bolívar was redenominated three times, removing three, five and then six zeros.

## What it kills
[[cash-and-fiat]], [[bank-deposits]] and [[bonds]] in that currency, along with pensions and salaries fixed in it. [[real-estate]], [[gold]], [[shares]] of companies that keep earning, and [[human-capital]] hold up better, but nothing is untouched by a collapsing economy.

> **Key rule:** a currency is a promise backed by trust in its issuer. Know which currency each of your stores is held in, and whether they're all the same one.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Can a central bank go bankrupt in its own currency?

**A:** Not in the ordinary sense: it can always create the money to pay what it owes. But the currency can fail as money if people stop trusting and accepting it.

**Q:** Venezuela removed zeros from its currency three times. Did that protect savers?

**A:** No. Removing zeros changes the units, not the value. Savings had already lost their buying power before each change.
