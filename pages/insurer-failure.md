## What fails
An **insurer or guarantor**: a firm that takes your money now in return for a promise to pay later. That covers life insurance, annuities that pay a pension for life, and bond insurers that guarantee other people's debts. Like a bank deposit, every policy is a [[claims-and-liabilities|claim]]: the insurer's liability, worth only what the insurer can pay.

## The backstop
- **Capital and reserves**, set by regulators so insurers can meet expected claims.
- **Guarantee schemes.** In the US, state guaranty associations cover policyholders of failed insurers up to limits set by each state. The most common limits are $300,000 for life insurance death benefits and $250,000 for annuities.
- **Government rescue,** when an insurer is big enough to threaten the system.

## Where the backstop runs out
- **Limits.** Large annuities and policies above the cap are only partly covered.
- **Promises cut, not cancelled.** A struggling insurer can reduce bonuses or benefits without formally failing.
- **Bond guarantees** are only as strong as the guarantor. When it falls, so does the rating of every bond it insured.
- **Rescues are discretionary.** AIG was saved because its failure threatened the system, not because policyholders were owed a rescue.

## Cases
- **Executive Life, 1991.** Once the largest life insurer in California, it became insolvent in April 1991 after heavy losses on junk bonds, and California took it over. At the time its parent was the largest US insurer ever to fail.
- **Equitable Life, 2000.** The UK's oldest mutual life insurer lost a House of Lords case on 20 July 2000 over guaranteed annuity rates it had promised. It closed to new business on 8 December 2000. In 2010 the government announced about £1.5 billion of compensation for policyholders.
- **AIG, 2008.** The insurer had sold huge amounts of protection against bond defaults. The US Federal Reserve and Treasury committed about $182 billion to stop it collapsing. By December 2012 the rescue had been fully repaid, with a positive return of about $22.7 billion.
- **Bond insurers, 2008–10.** Firms that guaranteed municipal and mortgage bonds lost their AAA ratings as mortgage losses mounted: Fitch cut Ambac in January 2008. Ambac's holding company filed for bankruptcy in November 2010.

## What it kills
Annuities and pensions paid by an insurer, the savings part of life policies, and [[bonds]] whose rating rested on a guarantee. It also hits [[human-capital]] protection, since disability and life cover are only as good as the insurer behind them, and the buildings cover that protects [[real-estate]].

> **Key rule:** an insurance policy is a long-dated promise. Check the insurer's strength and your guarantee scheme's limit before you rely on it for decades.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You hold a $400,000 annuity with an insurer that fails, in a state with a $250,000 annuity limit. What is at risk?

**A:** The $150,000 above the limit. The guaranty association covers up to $250,000; the rest depends on what is recovered from the failed insurer.

**Q:** AIG was rescued and taxpayers made a profit. Why is it still a warning?

**A:** Because the rescue was a choice made to protect the financial system. A smaller insurer, or a different government, might not have been saved.
