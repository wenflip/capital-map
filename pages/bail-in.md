## What it is
A **bail-out** rescues a bank with public money. A **bail-in** rescues it by imposing the losses on the bank's own investors and creditors: their claims are written down or turned into shares until the bank has enough capital again.

## Why it exists
After the 2008 crisis, many governments decided taxpayers shouldn't keep rescuing banks. Resolution laws — in the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada and elsewhere — now set out how a failing bank's own creditors absorb the losses first.

## How it works
Losses move up a **creditor ladder**. Each rung is wiped out before the next is touched:

| Order | Who takes the loss |
|---|---|
| 1 | Shareholders |
| 2 | Holders of the bank's riskiest bonds, issued to be written off in a crisis |
| 3 | Other junior bondholders |
| 4 | Senior bondholders and other unsecured creditors |
| 5 | Large deposits above the insurance limit (in many systems, those of individuals and small firms rank ahead of those of big companies) |
| — | **Insured deposits are protected** |

The exact order varies by country and by the bank's own legal terms.

**Real cases:**
- **Cyprus, 2013.** At Bank of Cyprus, 47.5% of large deposits above the €100,000 insured limit were turned into shares; at the second large bank, uninsured depositors bore most of the losses. Insured deposits were protected.
- **Credit Suisse, 2023.** In a government-arranged takeover by UBS, about 16 billion Swiss francs of the bank's riskiest bonds (AT1 bonds) were written to zero, while shareholders still received UBS shares — a controversial break with the usual order. In October 2025 Switzerland's Federal Administrative Court ruled that the write-off lacked a sufficient legal basis. The regulator and UBS appealed to the Federal Supreme Court, and as of early 2026 the bonds stayed written down while the appeal ran. A rule that looks clear on paper can still be tested in court for years.

## What goes wrong
- **Large depositors are creditors**, not owners of cash in a vault. Above the insurance limit, a deposit can be bailed in.
- **The order can be changed** by law or by the bond's own terms, as Credit Suisse bondholders found.
- **Contagion.** Fear of bail-in can trigger the very run it is meant to prevent.

> **Key rule:** above the insurance limit, a deposit is an unsecured loan to the bank, and in a failure it can be turned into shares or written down.

## Check yourself

**Q:** In a bail-in, who should take losses before large depositors?

**A:** Shareholders first, then holders of the bank's riskiest bonds, then other bondholders and unsecured creditors. Large deposits come near the end, and insured deposits are protected.
