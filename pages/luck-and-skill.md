## What it is
Luck is the part of an outcome that is not explained by a repeatable process. Skill is the part that is. In activities with a large role for chance, good results can come from bad decisions and bad results from good ones, especially over short periods. Separating the two requires looking at the process and at enough outcomes, not at a single result.

## Why it matters
People judge decisions by their outcomes. A fund that beat the market is called skilled. A trade that lost money is called a mistake. When luck is large, this confuses the quality of the decision with the quality of the result. Over many trials the process matters more. Over few trials the result is a poor guide.

## How it works
Ask whether the same process would be expected to produce similar results if repeated. In a coin toss the process has no skill; any streak is luck. In a market the process can have skill, but the outcome of any single period is still heavily influenced by chance. The longer the period and the more independent trials, the more the results reflect the process. Variance around the expected result is large when the edge is small. A skilled process can still lose for years. An unskilled process can still win for years.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** compare three managers over 10 years, counting the years each beats the market.
- With no skill at all (a 50% chance each year), the chance of beating the market in at least 6 of 10 years is about **37.7%**.
- With a small edge (52% a year), it's about **42.7%**.
- With a clear edge (55% a year), it's about **50.4%**.

The gap between no skill and a small edge is only about 5 percentage points. Ten years of results is often not enough to tell them apart.

## What goes wrong
- **Judging by the latest result.** One good or bad period is treated as proof of skill or its absence.
- **Ignoring the base rate.** Many participants will show good results by chance. The ones who did are the ones that are noticed.
- **Changing the process after a loss.** A sound process can produce losses. Abandoning it because of a short-term result replaces a known process with an unknown one.

> **Key rule:** judge the process by whether it would be expected to work if repeated, not by whether the last outcome was good.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Two managers have the same ten-year record. One followed a written process; the other changed approach after every loss. Which result is more informative?

**A:** The one with the consistent process. The same record from an inconsistent process is harder to attribute to skill rather than a sequence of lucky or unlucky choices.

**Q:** A strategy loses money for three years then gains it back. What does that show about skill?

**A:** Little by itself. Three years can be a normal drawdown for a skilled process or the start of failure for an unskilled one. The process and a longer record are needed.
