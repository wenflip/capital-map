## What it is
When you "have money in the bank", you don't own any specific cash. You hold a **claim on the bank**: its promise to pay you central bank money when you ask. A deposit is the bank's liability.

## Why it exists
Deposits are cheap to move and easy to use, and they let the bank lend. Most of the money in an economy is bank deposits, not notes, and most of it was created by bank lending.

## How it's protected
- **Deposit insurance.** Many countries guarantee deposits up to a set limit per person per bank. Above the limit, you are an ordinary creditor of the bank.
- **Regulation.** Banks must hold capital and liquid assets against their deposits.
- **The central bank** lends to sound banks in a panic, so deposits can be paid out.

The full chain, and how each link can break, is on [[backstops|When Banks Fail]].

## What goes wrong
- **Bank runs.** If many depositors want their money at once, a bank can't pay them all from the cash it holds.
- **Bail-ins.** In some resolution regimes, large uninsured deposits can be converted into shares or written down to rescue a failing bank.
- **Freezes and capital controls.** In a crisis, withdrawals can be limited.
- **Inflation**, as with all [[cash-and-fiat|cash]].

> **Key rule:** a deposit is a loan to your bank. Below the insurance limit, the state stands behind it; above it, the bank does.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why do bank runs move money *down* the hierarchy of money?

**A:** Depositors swap a bank's promise (a deposit) for central bank money (notes) or for deposits at a bank they trust more. See [[hierarchy-of-money]].
