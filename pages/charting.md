## What it is
**Charting** means reading price charts: how a price has moved over time, often with lines and indicators added. Charts summarise a lot of information quickly. They are good at showing what happened and poor at predicting what happens next.

## Why it exists
A chart shows at a glance the trend, the size of the swings and the drawdowns, things a table of numbers hides. Traders use charts to time decisions; long-term investors use them to understand history and risk.

## How it works
**Line and candlestick charts.** A line chart joins closing prices. A **candlestick** shows, for each period, the open, high, low and close: the "body" spans open to close, and thin "wicks" reach to the high and low.

**Linear or log scale.** On a linear scale, a rise from 100 to 200 looks ten times bigger than a rise from 10 to 20. On a **log scale**, both are doublings, so both look the same height. For long histories, log scale shows percentage changes honestly.

**Moving averages** smooth out noise. Fictional example: closing prices of 10, 11, 12, 13 and 14 over five days give a five-day moving average of **12**. A price above its long-term average is often read as an uptrend.

**Volume** shows how much traded, which helps judge whether a move had broad participation.

## What goes wrong
- **Seeing patterns in noise.** Random price series produce shapes that look like trends, "heads and shoulders" and channels. Many chart patterns don't hold up when tested.
- **The wrong scale.** Linear charts of long periods make early moves look tiny and recent ones huge.
- **Missing dividends.** Price charts leave out income, which understates long-run returns. See [[returns]].
- **Cherry-picked windows.** Where a chart starts and ends can change the story completely.

> **Key rule:** use charts to see what happened and how bumpy it was. Be sceptical of any chart that claims to show what happens next.

## Check yourself

**Q:** On a log-scale chart, a share's rise from 5 to 10 and its rise from 50 to 100 look the same height. Why?

**A:** Because both are doublings. A log scale gives equal height to equal percentage changes, not equal amounts.
