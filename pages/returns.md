## What it is
A **return** is how much an investment gained or lost over a period, as a percentage of what you started with. There are several versions, and mixing them up is one of the commonest mistakes in finance.

## Why it matters
"It returned 10%" can mean very different things: before or after inflation, with or without dividends, over one year or ten. Comparing investments only works when the returns measure the same thing.

## How it works
**Total return** counts income as well as price. Fictional example: you buy a share at 100, it pays a dividend of 3, and the price ends the year at 107. Total return = (107 − 100 + 3) ÷ 100 = **10%**.

**Real return** removes inflation. If the nominal return is 10% and prices rose 4%, the real return is 1.10 ÷ 1.04 − 1 = **5.77%**. Simply subtracting (10 − 4 = 6%) is close, but the gap grows when inflation is high. See [[inflation]].

**Annualised return** turns a multi-year result into a yearly rate. A gain of 50% over four years is not 12.5% a year; it is 1.50^(1/4) − 1 = **10.67%** a year, because each year's gain compounds on the last. See [[compounding]].

**Averages mislead.** An investment that rises 50% one year and falls 50% the next has an average return of 0%, but 100 becomes 150 and then 75: a loss of **25%**. Its annualised return is **−13.40%** a year.

| Year | Return | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Start | | 100 |
| 1 | +50% | 150 |
| 2 | −50% | 75 |

Volatility drags the compound return below the simple average. The bigger the swings, the bigger the drag.

## What goes wrong
- **Price-only figures** that leave out dividends understate shares' returns over long periods.
- **Nominal figures** in times of high inflation make poor results look good.
- **Simple averages** of yearly returns overstate what an investor actually earned.
- **The investor's own timing.** Money added after a good run and withdrawn after a fall can leave investors with less than the fund's published return.

> **Key rule:** compare total, real, annualised returns over the same period, after costs.

## Check yourself

**Q:** An investment doubles over ten years. Is that a 10% annual return?

**A:** No. It is about 7.18% a year: 2^(1/10) − 1. A steady 10% a year for ten years would turn 100 into about 259.
