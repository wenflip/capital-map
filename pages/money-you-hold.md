## What it is
The money you keep for spending, for emergencies and for safety. It comes in three main forms, and each is a different kind of promise:
- **Banknotes**, the cash in your wallet: a promise by the central bank, held by you directly.
- **Bank deposits**, the money "in the bank": a promise by your bank to pay you on demand.
- **Gold**, held as coins or bars: nobody's promise at all, just a metal people have treated as money for thousands of years.

How these forms are created, ranked and priced is covered in [[money|How Money Works]]. This branch is about holding them.

## Why it exists
Everyone holds some money, even people who own nothing else. It pays the bills, covers a shock without selling anything in a hurry, and waits for an opportunity. Most of the time the three forms feel the same: a number you can spend. They behave very differently when something goes wrong, and that difference is what this branch is for.

## How it works
- [[cash-and-fiat]] — government currency, why people must hold it, and its built-in bleed from inflation.
- [[bank-deposits]] — the money most people actually hold: a loan you have made to your bank.
- [[backstops]] — what stands behind a deposit when a bank fails: capital, the central bank, bail-in, deposit insurance and the government.
- [[gold]] — the oldest money, at the base of the hierarchy because it is nobody's liability.

| | Banknotes | Bank deposits | Gold |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Whose promise** | The central bank's | Your bank's | Nobody's |
| **What protects it** | You, physically | Deposit insurance, up to a limit | You, or a vault |
| **Main risk** | Inflation; theft | Bank failure above the limit; freezes | Price swings; theft |
| **Earns anything?** | No | Usually some interest | No |

Private dollar tokens sit beside these as a fourth form: see [[stablecoins]] in Digital Assets. They are promises by a private issuer, with no central bank or deposit insurance behind them.

On the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]], notes and gold are bearer assets you hold yourself, while deposits are on the record, protected by the state and exposed to it. The branch runs from cash in hand to the deposit system, then to gold, which sits outside it.

## What goes wrong
- **Inflation.** Cash and most deposits lose buying power every year that prices rise faster than the interest they earn. Over decades the loss is large. See [[inflation]].
- **Bank failure.** A deposit above the insurance limit is an unsecured loan to a failing bank. See [[backstops]].
- **Controls and freezes.** In a crisis, governments can limit withdrawals or transfers abroad. See [[capital-controls]].
- **Theft and loss.** Notes and gold held at home have no backstop at all if they are stolen.

> **Key rule:** "money in the bank" and "money in your hand" are different promises from different issuers. Know which one you hold, and what protects it.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You hold some banknotes, a bank deposit and a gold coin. Which of the three is nobody's liability, and what does that protect it from?

**A:** The gold coin. Because no one promised to pay it, no one's failure can wipe it out. That doesn't protect it from theft or from its price falling.

**Q:** Why can two people both "have money in the bank" and yet face very different risks?

**A:** Because the risk depends on the bank, and on how much each person holds there. A deposit within the insurance limit at a sound bank is far safer than a large deposit, well above the limit, at a weak one.

## Going deeper
- [[eurodollars]] — dollar deposits at banks outside the United States, which sit above ordinary deposits in the hierarchy and usually have no central bank behind them.
- **Money market funds** hold short-term government and bank debt and are often used like deposits, but they are funds, not deposits, and are usually not covered by deposit insurance.
