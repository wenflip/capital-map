## What it is
A time horizon is the period over which a decision must work. The same choice can be sensible over one period and poor over another. Cash that is needed next month has a different horizon from capital that will not be spent for twenty years.

## Why it matters
Results that look bad over a short period can be normal over a long one, and the reverse is also true. A process with a small edge can lose for several years. An asset that rises for a decade can still fall sharply in any single year. Judging a choice by the wrong horizon produces the wrong conclusion.

## How it works
State the period you can actually wait. Then ask what range of outcomes is normal for that period, not for a shorter or longer one. Short horizons make luck larger relative to any edge. Long horizons let compounding and mean reversion matter more, but only if you can stay invested through the intervening losses. Forced sales shorten the horizon whether you intended it or not.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** an asset averages 7% a year, with a typical yearly swing (standard deviation) of 15%. Treat each year as independent, with a normal spread of outcomes.
- **Over one year,** the chance of a loss is about **32%**.
- **Over twenty years,** the chance that the *average* year is a loss falls to about **1.8%**.
- **Yet over those same twenty years,** at least one losing year is close to certain: over **99%**.

The same asset is risky on a one-year view, and fairly dependable on a twenty-year view, but only for someone who can sit through the bad years along the way.

## What goes wrong
- **Using a short-term result to judge a long-term choice.** One bad year is treated as proof the process failed.
- **Pretending a short horizon is long.** Money that may be needed soon cannot safely be treated as if it has decades.
- **Ignoring forced exits.** Leverage, redemptions or spending needs can turn a long intended horizon into a short realised one.

> **Key rule:** decide the horizon first, then judge the choice against the range of outcomes that is normal for that horizon.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A strategy is expected to work over ten years but has lost money for three. What does the horizon tell you?

**A:** Three years is inside the range a ten-year process can produce. The horizon says the three-year result is not yet evidence the process failed, provided you can still wait the full period.

**Q:** You may need the money in eighteen months. How should that change the choice?

**A:** The horizon is short. Outcomes that would be acceptable over decades may not be acceptable if a loss in the next eighteen months would force a sale.
