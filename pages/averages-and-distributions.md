## What it is
An average is a single number that summarises a set of outcomes. The distribution is the full set: how many outcomes are high, how many are low, and how far they sit from the centre. The average can stay the same while the distribution changes, and one extreme value can shift the average while leaving most outcomes unchanged.

## Why it matters
Decisions are often made on the average alone. A fund that returns 8% on average can still lose money in many years. A group whose average wealth rises because one member becomes very rich has not made the typical member richer. Looking only at the average misses the range you actually face and the typical case.

## How it works
Ask what the average is made of. Is it pulled by a few large outcomes, or is it close to most of the outcomes? The mean (total divided by count) is sensitive to extremes. The median (the middle value) is not. When the distribution is skewed, the mean and the median diverge. A wide distribution means the average is a poor guide to any single result.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** nine people each have 50,000. One person has 10,000,000. The mean wealth is (9 × 50,000 + 10,000,000) / 10 = **1,045,000**. The median is **50,000**. The average rose because of one person; the typical person did not.

## What goes wrong
- **Treating the average as the typical result.** Most outcomes can sit far from the mean.
- **Ignoring skew.** One large gain or loss moves the mean while leaving the rest of the distribution unchanged.
- **Comparing averages across different spreads.** Two averages can be equal while the chance of a large loss is very different.

> **Key rule:** before using an average, ask what the distribution looks like and whether one extreme is doing the work.

## Check yourself

**Q:** The average return of a strategy is 10% a year. In half the years it loses 20% and in half it gains 40%. Is 10% a good description of a typical year?

**A:** No. A typical year is either −20% or +40%. The 10% average is the result of those two outcomes (0.5 × −20 + 0.5 × 40 = 10), not a result that occurs often.

**Q:** Adding one very large observation raises the mean but not the median. What does that tell you?

**A:** The rise in the average is driven by the extreme value. Most of the observations are unchanged.
