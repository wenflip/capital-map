## What it is
**Data sources** are where the numbers behind investment decisions come from: prices, company accounts, economic statistics and blockchain activity. The quality of any analysis is limited by the quality of its data.

## Why it matters
Two analyses of the "same" data can reach different answers if one uses revised figures and the other doesn't, or if one leaves out companies that went bust. Knowing where data comes from, and how it can mislead, is part of reading any chart or claim.

## How it works
| Kind | Primary sources | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Prices** | Exchanges, index providers | Delays, missing dividends, unadjusted splits |
| **Company accounts** | Filings with regulators, such as the US SEC's EDGAR database | Restatements, one-off items |
| **Economic data** | Central banks and national statistics offices; aggregators such as the St. Louis Fed's FRED | Revisions after first release |
| **On-chain data** | Block explorers and the blockchain itself | Labels and estimates layered on top of raw data |

**Prefer the primary source.** A figure from the statistics office or the filing itself beats one copied through several websites.

**Check the basics:** units, currency, dates, whether prices include dividends, and whether figures are adjusted for inflation or seasons.

## What goes wrong
- **Survivorship bias.** A database of today's funds or companies leaves out those that closed or failed, which makes past returns look better than they were.
- **Look-ahead bias.** Testing a strategy with data that wasn't available at the time, such as revised economic figures.
- **Revisions.** First estimates of growth or jobs are often revised, sometimes a lot.
- **Silent errors.** A bad tick, a missing day or a mis-scaled number can distort a whole analysis.
- **Unverified on-chain labels.** Which address belongs to whom is often an estimate.

> **Key rule:** go to the primary source, read the definitions, and ask what's missing from the data before trusting what's in it.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A database of today's funds shows excellent average ten-year returns. Why might that overstate what investors earned?

**A:** Survivorship bias. Funds that did badly and closed over those ten years aren't in today's database, so the average leaves out the worst results.
