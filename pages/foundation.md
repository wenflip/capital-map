## What it is
The handful of ideas that every other branch of the map uses. They don't depend on any particular asset. A bond, a house and a Bitcoin are all judged with the same tools: who owes what, what money is worth over time, how returns build up, how wide the range of outcomes is, and what rising prices do to it all.

## Why it exists
Most costly money mistakes aren't about picking the wrong asset. They come from misreading one of these basics:
- treating a promise as if it were the thing itself;
- comparing money today with money in ten years as if they were equal;
- underestimating what small differences in return or fees do over decades;
- confusing a smooth ride with a safe one;
- forgetting that a 5% return in a year of 4% inflation is barely a gain.

Four pages explain how value moves through time:

- [[compounding]] — returns earning returns: why time matters more than timing, and why fees matter more than they look.

## What goes wrong
| Mistake | What it looks like | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Ignoring whose promise it is | "Money in the bank is safe, full stop" | Ask who owes it and what happens if they can't pay |
| Adding up money from different years | "I paid 200,000 and sold for 300,000, so I made 100,000" | Discount or adjust for inflation before comparing |
| Underrating small percentages | "A 1% fee is nothing" | Compound it over the holding period |
| Treating low volatility as low risk | "It never moves, so it can't fail" | Look for the killer, not just the wobble |
| Quoting nominal returns | "Cash paid 4% this year" | Subtract inflation to get the real return |

> **Key rule:** compare like with like. Money at different times, in different units or with different risks isn't the same money until you adjust for it.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Two investments both return 7% a year. One charges a 1% annual fee, the other 0.2%. Why does the gap matter more over 30 years than over 1 year?

**A:** Because the fee comes out every year, and each year's lost money would itself have earned returns. Over one year the gap is under 1% of the balance; over 30 years it compounds into a much larger share of the final value. See [[compounding]].

**Q:** Your savings earned 3% last year while prices rose 5%. Did your wealth grow?

**A:** In dollars, yes; in what those dollars can buy, no. The real return was about −2%, so your purchasing power fell. See [[inflation]].

## Going deeper
- **Every valuation is time value plus risk.** A price is the future cash an asset is expected to pay, discounted at a rate that reflects both waiting and uncertainty. The pages on [[time-value-of-money]] and [[risk-and-return]] are the two halves of that rate.
- **Losses and gains aren't symmetrical.** A fall of 50% needs a rise of 100% to recover. This is why the spread of outcomes, not just the average, decides long-run results; see the advanced section on [[risk-and-return]].
