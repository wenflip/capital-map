## What it is
Active management is the attempt to choose securities or time markets so as to beat a benchmark. William Sharpe's 1991 argument shows why, as a matter of arithmetic, the average actively managed dollar must earn less than the average passively managed dollar after costs.

## Why it matters
The claim does not depend on skill, efficiency or any particular market. It follows from the fact that active investors as a group hold the market, and that their trading and fees are a cost the passive holders do not pay. The arithmetic sets a baseline: any outperformance by some active investors must be matched by underperformance by others, before costs, and the average after costs must be lower.

## How it works
Sharpe's argument has two steps, and both are about the average **dollar**, not the average investor:
1. **Passive investors hold every security in its market weight,** so before costs they earn exactly the market return.
2. **The market return is the average of the active and passive parts together.** If the passive part earns the market return, the active part, taken as a whole, must earn it too, before costs.

Active management costs more: research, trading and higher fees. So **after costs, the average active dollar must earn less than the average passive dollar.** The argument doesn't say no active investor can outperform. It says the active group as a whole can't, after costs.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** the market returns 8% before costs. Passive funds cost 0.1% a year and active funds 1%. After costs the average passive dollar earns 8% − 0.1% = **7.9%**, and the average active dollar 8% − 1% = **7%**. Some active funds will beat 7.9%; for that to happen, others must fall below 7%.

**The other side.** In 2018, Lasse Heje Pedersen argued that Sharpe's arithmetic assumes the market never changes. In practice companies issue and buy back shares and indices change, so even passive investors must trade, and active investors can in principle add value on those trades. Most agree the core point about costs still stands; the debate is over how exact it is.

## What goes wrong
- **Treating the argument as a forecast of any single manager.** It constrains the average, not every participant.
- **Ignoring costs when comparing.** Gross-of-fee outperformance can become net-of-fee underperformance.
- **Assuming the passive alternative is costless.** Passive vehicles also have costs, but they are typically lower; the gap remains.

> **Key rule:** before costs the average active investor matches the market; after costs the average trails it by the amount of those costs.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Passive investors earn the market return before costs. Why does that force the same result on active investors as a group?

**A:** Because the market return is the average of everyone's holdings. If the passive part earns exactly the market return, the active part as a whole must earn it too, before costs.

**Q:** Why does the average active investor trail after costs even if some active investors beat the market?

**A:** Because the outperformance of some is offset by the underperformance of others before costs, and the costs themselves reduce the average for the group.
