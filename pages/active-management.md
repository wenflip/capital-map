## What it is
Active management is the attempt to choose securities or time markets so as to beat a benchmark. William Sharpe’s 1991 argument shows why, as a matter of arithmetic, the average active investor must underperform the market after costs.

## Why it matters
The claim does not depend on skill, efficiency or any particular market. It follows from the fact that active investors as a group hold the market, and that their trading and fees are a cost the passive holders do not pay. The arithmetic sets a baseline: any outperformance by some active investors must be matched by underperformance by others, before costs, and the average after costs must be lower.

## How it works
Before costs, the return of all investors weighted by the amounts they hold equals the market return. Active investors are a subset. Their aggregate holdings, before costs, must also equal the market return; otherwise the remaining (passive) investors would have to hold something other than the market. After costs — management fees, trading costs, taxes — the average active investor therefore trails the market by the amount of those costs. The argument does not say that no active investor can outperform. It says that the average, after costs, cannot.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** the market returns 8%. Active investors in aggregate hold the same securities. Their average cost is 1% a year. Their average return is therefore 7%. Some may return more than 8%; others must return less, so that the average remains 7%.

## What goes wrong
- **Treating the argument as a forecast of any single manager.** It constrains the average, not every participant.
- **Ignoring costs when comparing.** Gross-of-fee outperformance can become net-of-fee underperformance.
- **Assuming the passive alternative is costless.** Passive vehicles also have costs, but they are typically lower; the gap remains.

> **Key rule:** before costs the average active investor matches the market; after costs the average trails it by the amount of those costs.

## Check yourself

**Q:** If active investors as a group hold the market portfolio, what must their average return be before costs?

**A:** The market return. Any other result would require the remaining investors to hold something other than the market.

**Q:** Why does the average active investor trail after costs even if some active investors beat the market?

**A:** Because the outperformance of some is offset by the underperformance of others before costs, and the costs themselves reduce the average for the group.
