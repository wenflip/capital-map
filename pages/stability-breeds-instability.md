## What it is
The economist Hyman Minsky argued that a financial system doesn't just get hit by crises from outside. It walks into them. In a long stretch of good times, borrowers and lenders grow confident, borrow more, and take on debts that only work if the good times continue. The calm makes the system fragile. He set this out as the **financial instability hypothesis** in 1992.

## Why it matters
It explains a pattern that otherwise looks strange: the biggest crashes tend to follow the longest, calmest booms, not the bumpy periods. And it warns that the moment risk feels lowest can be the moment it is highest.

## How it works
**Three kinds of borrower.** Minsky sorted borrowers by how they pay their debts. In plain terms:
- **Safe borrowers** earn enough to pay both the interest and the debt itself.
- **Rollover borrowers** earn enough for the interest, but need to borrow again to repay the debt when it falls due.
- **Bet-on-prices borrowers** can't even cover the interest. They rely on the asset they bought rising in price.

**The drift.** In a long calm, lenders relax and prices rise, so the mix shifts from the first kind towards the second and third. Nothing breaks while prices rise and credit flows.

**The turn.** Then something small changes: rates rise, or one lender gets nervous. The third kind must sell, which pushes prices down, which turns the second kind into the third. The point where this tips is often called a **Minsky moment**.

**Fictional example.** Three borrowers each owe 100,000 at 6% interest, so each owes 6,000 a year in interest. One has 15,000 a year spare, one has 6,000, and one has 3,000.
- At 6%, the first pays the interest and 9,000 off the debt; the second just covers the interest; the third falls 3,000 short and relies on prices rising.
- If the rate rises to 8%, interest becomes 8,000. Now the second borrower is 2,000 short too.

## What goes wrong
- **Calm read as safety.** Risk measures built on recent history say risk is low exactly when borrowing is building. See [[volatility]].
- **Everyone tips together.** The borrowers are linked through the same prices and lenders, so the fall spreads. See [[forced-sellers]].
- **Rescues reset the clock.** Each rescue shows that crises can be survived, which can encourage the next round of borrowing.

> **Key rule:** a long calm is not evidence of safety. Ask who has borrowed against the calm continuing, and what happens to them if it ends.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A borrower owes 100,000 and has 7,000 a year to spend on interest. At what interest rate do they stop covering it?

**A:** Above **7%**: 7% of 100,000 is 7,000 a year. Any higher rate turns them from a rollover borrower into one betting on prices.

**Q:** Why can a long period of low volatility make a crash worse?

**A:** Because calm encourages people to borrow more and hold riskier positions, and risk models built on the calm allow it. When prices finally move, more people are forced to sell at once.
