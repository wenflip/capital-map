## What it is
**Eurodollars** are US dollar deposits and loans held at banks outside the United States. The name is historical: the market started in Europe, but today it is global.

A eurodollar is a **promise by a foreign bank** to pay dollars. Such banks usually have no direct access to the US central bank's lending, so they must find dollars in the market when they need them.

## Why it matters
- **Huge dollar debts outside the US.** Companies and governments around the world borrow in dollars. They must earn or buy dollars to repay, which is a large source of global demand for the currency.
- **Dollar shortages.** In a crisis, everyone owing dollars scrambles for them at once, and the dollar tends to rise even when the crisis started in the US. See [[exchange-rates]].
- **Swap lines.** The US central bank can lend dollars to other central banks in emergencies, which then lend them on to their banks.

## Where it sits
In the [[hierarchy-of-money|hierarchy of money]], eurodollars sit above [[bank-deposits]] at US banks: a private, offshore promise to pay dollars, usually without a direct central bank backstop.

> **Key rule:** much of the world's dollar system runs outside the United States, which is why a global dollar squeeze can hit markets everywhere at once.
