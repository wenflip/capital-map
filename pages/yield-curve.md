## What it is
The **yield curve** is a line showing the yields on one borrower's bonds — usually a government's — across different maturities, from a few months to thirty years or more.

## Why it matters
The curve sums up what markets expect from interest rates and the economy. It sets the base for loan and mortgage rates of every length, and its shape is one of the most watched signals in finance.

## How it works
Long-term yields reflect two things:
- **Expected short-term rates** over the life of the bond.
- **A term premium:** extra yield for tying money up longer and carrying more [[bond-pricing|price risk]].

| Shape | What it looks like | Usual reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal (upward-sloping)** | Long yields above short yields | Lenders want more for lending longer; growth expected |
| **Flat** | Similar yields at all lengths | Uncertainty; a turning point |
| **Inverted** | Short yields above long yields | Markets expect rates to fall, often because they expect a slowdown |

**The short end** is anchored by the central bank's policy rate. **The long end** is set by the market, and by central banks when they buy or sell long-dated bonds. See [[monetary-policy]] and [[central-bank-money]].

## What goes wrong
- **Reading it as a forecast.** In the United States, an inverted curve has often come before recessions, but not every time, and the delay has varied widely. It's a signal, not a schedule.
- **Borrowing short to lend long.** Banks and others that fund long-dated assets with short-term money get squeezed when short rates rise above long ones.
- **Distortion.** Large central-bank bond buying can push long yields down, muting the signal.

> **Key rule:** the yield curve is the market's view of future rates, plus a reward for waiting. Read its shape as a mood, not a prophecy.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Short-term yields are 5% and ten-year yields are 4%. What shape is the curve, and what does it usually suggest?

**A:** Inverted. It usually suggests markets expect short-term rates to fall in future, often because they expect the economy to slow.
