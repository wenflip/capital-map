## What it is
**Return** is what an investment gains or loses. **Risk** is the uncertainty around that result: how wide the range of possible outcomes is, and how bad the worst ones are.

## Why it exists
People need to be paid to accept uncertainty. If a risky asset offered the same expected return as a safe one, nobody would hold the risky one. So, on average and over long periods, riskier assets have to offer higher expected returns. The extra return over a safe asset is called the **risk premium**.

That is a statement about averages and expectations, not a promise. Taking more risk raises the *range* of outcomes, including the bad ones.

## How it works
- **The safe benchmark.** Short-term government debt such as [[t-bills]] is usually treated as the near risk-free rate. Everything else is judged against it.
- **Measuring the spread.** The most common measure is [[volatility]]: how widely returns swing around their average. It is useful, but it isn't the whole story.
- **Two kinds of risk:**
  - *Specific risk* belongs to one company or asset (a failed product, a fraud). It can be reduced by [[diversification]].
  - *Market-wide risk* hits almost everything at once (a recession, a liquidity squeeze). It can't be diversified away, which is why it is the risk that tends to be rewarded.
- **Losses and gains aren't symmetrical.**

| Fall | Gain needed to get back to the start |
|---|---|
| −20% | +25% |
| −50% | +100% |
| −80% | +400% |

## What goes wrong
- **Mistaking a smooth ride for safety.** An asset that rarely moves can still fail completely. The [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]] names each store's killer for this reason.
- **Reaching for return** without asking what extra risk comes with it.
- **Holding one big position**, so a single failure decides everything. See [[position-sizing]].

> **Key rule:** risk is not how much a price wobbles — it is the chance of a loss you can't recover from. Volatility is one clue to it.

## Check yourself

**Q:** An investment rises 50% in year one and falls 50% in year two. What is the total result?

**A:** A loss of 25%. 100 becomes 150, then 75. The average of the two yearly returns is 0%, but the result is negative.

**Q:** Why isn't company-specific risk usually rewarded with a higher expected return?

**A:** Because it can be removed cheaply by holding many companies. Investors aren't paid for a risk they don't need to carry.

## Going deeper
- **Volatility drag.** When returns vary, the compound (geometric) average return is lower than the simple (arithmetic) average. The bigger the swings, the bigger the gap. This is why two investments with the same average yearly return can end with very different wealth, and why reducing large losses can raise long-run growth.
- **Risk is a property of the whole portfolio.** What matters is how an asset moves alongside everything else you own, not how risky it is alone. See [[correlation]].
