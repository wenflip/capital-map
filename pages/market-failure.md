## What fails
The **market**: the place where you can sell. A share or bond is only worth what someone will pay. If the exchange shuts, if a whole market is forced to sell at once, or if the system it belongs to disappears, the price on your statement stops meaning anything.

## The backstop
- **Central banks** can buy assets to stop a fire sale, standing in for dealers who are already full. See [[lender-of-last-resort]] and [[market-making]].
- **Circuit breakers and closures** pause trading to stop panic. See [[exchanges]].
- **Time.** Most markets that fell have eventually recovered, though sometimes over decades.

## Where the backstop runs out
- **You can't sell while it's shut.** A closure protects prices, not your access to cash.
- **Recovery can outlast you.** A market that takes thirty years to regain its peak isn't much help to someone who needs the money in ten.
- **Some markets never came back.** And the records most people study come from markets that survived.

## Cases
- **New York, 1914.** As the First World War began, the New York Stock Exchange closed on 31 July 1914 and didn't reopen for stocks until 12 December, more than four months later.
- **Markets that vanished.** After the Russian revolution of 1917 and the Communist victory in China in 1949, shareholders lost everything. Philippe Jorion and William Goetzmann studied 39 markets over the 20th century: the US had the highest uninterrupted real return, about 4.7% a year, against a median of about 1.5% for the others. Long-run "stocks always win" figures lean on the market that survived.
- **Japan, 1989–2024.** The Nikkei 225's 1989 closing high wasn't beaten until 22 February 2024. See [[shares]].
- **UK gilts, September 2022.** 30-year government bond yields rose by 130 basis points in three trading days. Pension funds using leveraged strategies faced collateral calls and had to sell gilts, which pushed prices down further. The Bank of England bought £19.3 billion of gilts between 28 September and 14 October to stop the spiral.
- **Moscow, 2022.** Share trading stopped after 25 February and restarted on 24 March, with foreign investors barred from selling.

## What it kills
Everything that relies on a sale to turn into money: [[shares]], [[bonds]], [[funds-and-etfs|funds]] and [[collectibles]]. Even "safe" government bonds, as the gilt crisis showed, can fall fast when forced sellers meet no buyers. See [[liquidity]].

> **Key rule:** a price is only real if you can sell at it. Keep money you'll need soon in a form that doesn't depend on a market being open.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why does the long-run return of US shares overstate what a global investor could expect?

**A:** Survivorship. The US was the most successful market of the century; others had lower returns, and some closed for good. Studying only the survivor makes the past look safer than it was.

**Q:** In 2022, why did UK pension funds sell government bonds into a falling market?

**A:** Their leveraged strategies needed more collateral as bond prices fell, so they had to sell bonds to raise cash, which pushed prices down further.
