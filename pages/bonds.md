## What it is
A **bond** is a loan broken into tradable pieces. The borrower (the **issuer**) promises to pay interest at set times and repay the loan on a set date.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Face value** | The amount repaid at the end (often 100 or 1,000 per bond) |
| **Coupon** | The interest rate on the face value, paid regularly |
| **Maturity** | The date the face value is repaid |
| **Issuer** | Who owes the money: a government, a company, a city |

## Why it exists
Bonds let borrowers raise large sums for long periods from many lenders at once, and let those lenders sell to someone else instead of waiting for repayment.

## How it works
**Main kinds of issuer:**
- **Governments** borrowing in their own currency: usually the lowest-risk bonds in that currency.
- **Local governments and agencies:** backed by taxes or by specific projects.
- **Companies:** higher yields to pay for the risk that the company fails. See [[credit-risk]].

**Main kinds of bond:**
- **Fixed-rate:** the same coupon every period.
- **Floating-rate:** the coupon resets with a benchmark interest rate.
- **Zero-coupon:** no coupons; bought at a discount and repaid at face value, like a long [[t-bills|T-bill]].
- **Inflation-linked:** payments rise with prices. See [[inflation-linked-bonds]].

**Where bondholders stand.** If a company fails, bondholders are paid before shareholders. Secured bonds, backed by specific assets, are paid before unsecured ones. See [[claims-and-liabilities]].

## What goes wrong
- **Default:** the issuer misses payments. Bondholders may get back only part of what they're owed.
- **Rate rises** cut the market price. See [[bond-pricing]].
- **Inflation** erodes fixed payments.
- **Early repayment:** some bonds can be repaid early by the issuer, usually when rates have fallen and you'd least want your money back.

> **Key rule:** a bond is a promise. Judge the promiser first, then the price.

## How it has failed
- **Repudiation, 1918.** After the revolution, Russia's new government refused to honour the Tsarist state's debts. Foreign bondholders lost almost everything.
- **Argentina, 2001.** Argentina defaulted on its government debt, then the largest sovereign default on record, and has restructured its debt several times since.
- **Greece, 2012.** Private holders of Greek government bonds took a 53.5% cut to the face value of their bonds, reducing Greece's debt by about €107 billion.
- **Inflation.** Bonds can fail without a default. In the high inflation of the 1970s, holders of long-dated bonds were repaid in full in money that bought far less. See [[inflation]].

## Check yourself

**Q:** A company fails. Who is paid first: its bondholders or its shareholders?

**A:** Its bondholders. Shareholders own what's left only after every creditor, including bondholders, has been paid.
