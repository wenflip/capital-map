## What it is
When people say something is **priced in**, they mean the market already expected it, so the current price already reflects it. A price is a running bet on the future. By the time the news arrives, much of it has usually been bet on already.

So prices don't move on whether news is good or bad. They move on whether it is **better or worse than expected**.

## Why it matters
It explains the most confusing thing newcomers see: a company reports record profits and its shares fall; a central bank cuts rates and markets drop; a country announces a recession and shares rise. In each case the news was compared with what was already expected, and fell short or beat it.

## How it works
**Expectations are measurable.** Analysts publish forecasts, and the average forecast (the **consensus**) is what results are judged against. For interest rates, prices in the futures market show what traders expect the central bank to do. See [[futures]] and [[monetary-policy]].

**Fictional example.** A company's shares trade at 50. The market expects profit of 2.00 per share and values the company at 25 times its profit: 25 × 2.00 = 50.
- The company reports 1.80, and the market lowers its expectations to match. At the same multiple: 25 × 1.80 = **45**, a **10%** fall, even though the company made a healthy profit.
- If it had reported 2.20 and expectations rose to match: 25 × 2.20 = 55, a 10% rise.

See [[valuation]] for where the multiple comes from.

**"Buy the rumour, sell the news."** Traders buy while good news is expected, pushing the price up. When the news finally arrives, there is no surprise left and no one new to buy, so the price can fall even though the news was good.

## What goes wrong
- **Reacting to headlines.** By the time news reaches the public, professional traders have usually acted on it.
- **Crowded expectations.** When everyone expects the same thing, the risk is in the surprise nobody prepared for.
- **Changing the yardstick.** Expectations shift as news arrives, so a "beat" in the morning can look like a miss by the afternoon.

> **Key rule:** don't ask "is this news good?" Ask "was it better or worse than what was already expected?"

## Check yourself

**Q:** A company's shares trade at 15 times expected profit of 4.00 per share. It reports 4.40, and the market raises its expectations to match while keeping the same multiple. What is the new price, and the change?

**A:** 15 × 4.00 = 60 before; 15 × 4.40 = **66** after, a rise of **10%**.

**Q:** A central bank cuts interest rates and shares fall the same day. How can that happen?

**A:** The market may have expected a bigger cut, or read the cut as a sign the central bank sees trouble ahead. The price moved on the gap between the decision and what was priced in.
