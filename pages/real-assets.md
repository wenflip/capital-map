## What it is
**Real assets** are physical things with value of their own: land and buildings, raw materials, and objects people collect. Unlike a deposit, bond or share, a real asset isn't anyone's promise to pay. If you hold it directly, nobody owes it to you, so nobody can default on it.
- [[real-estate]] — land and buildings: shelter, rent and leverage.
- [[reits]] — companies that own property, traded like shares.
- [[commodities]] — oil, grain and metals, traded in bulk.
- [[collectibles]] — art, watches, wine and other objects valued by what a buyer will pay.

[[gold]] is a real asset too, but it lives on the Money branch because of its long history as money.

## Why it exists
People hold real assets for three reasons:
- **Use.** You can live in a house, burn fuel, eat grain or hang a painting. Their value doesn't depend only on someone else's promise.
- **Inflation.** When money loses value, the price of physical things tends to rise in money terms, so real assets can hold their worth better than fixed payments. The protection is uneven and slow; it is not a guarantee. See [[inflation]].
- **A different set of risks.** Their prices are driven partly by supply, weather, location and taste, not only by interest rates and company profits, which can make them useful alongside bonds and shares. See [[diversification]].

## How it works
Every real asset can be held in one of two ways, and they land in very different places on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]].

| Held as | What you own | Example | Whose risk? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The thing itself** | The asset | A house you hold title to, a painting, bars of metal | Yours: storage, upkeep, theft, the state |
| **A claim on it** | A share, fund unit or contract | A REIT, a commodity fund, a futures contract | The issuer, the fund and the market as well |

Holding the thing gives you control but costs effort: storage, insurance, maintenance, and a slow, expensive sale. Holding a claim is cheap and easy to trade, but you've swapped part of the asset's risk for counterparty risk. It's the same choice as physical gold against a gold fund.

Most real assets **pay nothing for being held**, apart from rented property. Their return comes from price changes, so it depends on what the next buyer will pay.

## What goes wrong
- **Illiquidity.** Selling a house or a painting can take months, and a forced sale usually means a lower price.
- **Holding costs.** Repairs, storage, insurance and taxes are paid every year whether the price rises or not.
- **Price swings.** Commodity prices can collapse when supply jumps or demand falls; property can fall for years after a boom.
- **The state.** Land can't be moved, so it is the easiest asset to tax, regulate or seize. That is why real estate sits at the most legible corner of the chart.

> **Key rule:** a real asset removes the borrower's promise but adds the asset's own costs. Count storage, upkeep and the cost of selling before you count the gain.

## Check yourself

**Q:** You own a house outright and also own shares in a REIT. Which is a claim and which isn't?

**A:** The REIT share is a claim: you own part of a company that owns buildings. The house isn't anyone's liability; you own the asset itself, recorded in a land registry.

**Q:** Why are real assets said to protect against inflation only "partly"?

**A:** Because their prices also depend on supply, demand, interest rates and location. Over long periods they often keep up with prices, but over short ones they can fall while inflation is high.

## Going deeper
- **Real assets and interest rates.** Most real assets pay little or nothing, so when interest rates rise, holding them costs more in lost interest. Property prices are especially sensitive, because most property is bought with a mortgage.
- **Appraisal smoothing.** Private property funds value buildings by appraisal a few times a year, not by daily trading. Their prices look calmer than REITs that hold similar buildings, but the risk is the same; it just shows up later.
