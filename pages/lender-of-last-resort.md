## What it is
A central bank acts as **lender of last resort** when it lends to banks that can't borrow anywhere else during a panic. Because it creates its own currency, it can't run out of it.

## Why it exists
A run can sink a bank that is fundamentally sound, simply because its loans can't be sold fast enough. Emergency lending turns those loans into cash so depositors can be paid, and the panic burns out.

## How it works
The classic rule comes from the economist Walter Bagehot in 1873. In a panic the central bank should:
- **lend freely**, so nobody doubts cash is available;
- **against good collateral**, so it only lends to banks that are solvent;
- **at a penalty rate**, so banks don't use it in normal times.

Modern central banks run standing facilities on these lines, and in big crises also lend to other central banks through **swap lines**. See [[eurodollars]].

## What goes wrong
- **Telling illiquid from insolvent** is hard in a crisis. Lending to a bank that is actually insolvent just delays the loss and may move it to the public.
- **Stigma.** Banks may avoid borrowing in case markets take it as a sign of trouble.
- **Foreign-currency debts.** A central bank can create only its own currency; banks owing dollars elsewhere depend on the US central bank's swap lines.

> **Key rule:** the central bank can cure a shortage of cash, not a shortage of capital.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why does the classic rule say to lend at a penalty rate?

**A:** So that banks use the central bank only in a real emergency, not as a cheap everyday source of funding.
