## What it is
**Social capital** is the value of who knows you and trusts you: your reputation, your network, and the goodwill you have built by delivering on promises. It is why a known plumber gets the next job without an advert, and why a founder with a track record raises money more easily than one without.

## Why it matters
- **It opens doors that money can't:** introductions, references, first customers, a second chance after a failure.
- **It lowers costs.** People who trust you need fewer contracts, guarantees and checks.
- **It works with human capital.** Skill gets you the job; reputation gets you the chance to show the skill.

## How it's built
- **Slowly, by delivering.** Every kept promise adds a little; there is no quick way to buy it.
- **By helping first.** Networks grow by giving introductions and help, not only by asking for them.
- **By being consistent.** Trust comes from behaving the same way whether or not anyone is watching.

It is only **partly transferable**. You can introduce someone, recommend them or bring a child into a family business, but you can't sell your reputation or hand it over whole.

## What goes wrong
- **Betrayal or a reputation collapse**, the killer on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]: decades to build, gone in a day.
- **Drift:** relationships that aren't maintained fade.
- **A shared fall:** a reputation tied to one employer, partner or scene can sink with it.

> **Key rule:** social capital is earned slowly and lost quickly. Protect it the way you would protect your savings.

## Connects to

## How it has failed
- **A firm's fall becomes yours.** The accounting firm Arthur Andersen collapsed in 2002 after the Enron scandal. Its conviction was overturned by the US Supreme Court in 2005, but by then the firm had gone, and its people's professional reputations were tied to its name.
- **Networks scatter.** When a company or industry shrinks, the colleagues, customers and contacts that made up your network disperse with it.
- **One scandal.** A single public mistake, or being associated with someone else's, can undo decades of trust.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why is social capital described as "partly transferable"?

**A:** Because you can pass on introductions and recommendations, but the trust itself belongs to you. The person you introduce still has to earn their own.
