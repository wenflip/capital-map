## What it is
**Inflation** is a general rise in prices across the economy, so each unit of money buys less. It is usually measured with a price index that tracks the cost of a typical basket of goods and services, such as a consumer price index.

The headline basket rises 3.7%, a renter's basket 4.3%, and a homeowner who drives to work 3.1%.](assets/figures/inflation-your-basket.svg "Three fictional households facing the same price rises. Each household&#39;s rate is its own basket weighted by what it spends.")

## Why it matters
Inflation is the killer of [[cash-and-fiat|cash]] on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]. It also changes how every other return should be read: a gain only counts if it beats the rise in prices.

**Worked example (fictional figures):** at 3% inflation a year, after 20 years each unit of money buys only about **55%** of what it did at the start.

## How it works
**Nominal versus real.**
- A **nominal** return is the number on the statement.
- A **real** return is what's left after inflation: the change in what your money can actually buy.

A quick estimate is *real ≈ nominal − inflation*. The exact figure is (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. At 5% nominal and 3% inflation the real return is about **1.94%**.

**What drives it.** Economists point to several causes, often acting together:
- **Demand-pull:** spending grows faster than the economy can supply goods.
- **Cost-push:** a key cost such as energy or wages rises and is passed on.
- **Money and credit growth** outpacing output. See [[central-bank-money]] and [[money-creation]].
- **Expectations:** if people expect higher prices, they ask for higher wages and set higher prices, which helps make it happen.

**The policy response.** Many central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often around 2% a year, mainly by raising or lowering interest rates.

## Who wins and who loses
| Usually loses | Usually gains or is protected |
|---|---|
| Holders of cash and fixed-rate savings | Borrowers with fixed-rate debt (repaid in cheaper money) |
| Lenders and holders of fixed-rate [[bonds]] | Owners of assets whose income can rise with prices, such as some [[real-estate]] and businesses |
| People on fixed incomes | Holders of inflation-linked bonds |

## What goes wrong
- **Reading nominal gains as real ones.** A house that "doubled" over a long period may have gained little after inflation.
- **High and unstable inflation** makes planning, pricing and saving hard, and can end in a currency crisis.
- **Deflation**, a general fall in prices, causes its own problems: debts become heavier in real terms, and people delay spending.

> **Key rule:** always ask whether a return is nominal or real. Only the real return tells you whether you are richer.

## Connects to

## Check yourself

**Q:** Your savings account paid 4% while inflation was 6%. What was your approximate real return?

**A:** About −2% (the exact figure is about −1.9%). You have more money, but it buys less.

**Q:** Why do fixed-rate borrowers tend to benefit from unexpected inflation?

**A:** Because the debt is fixed in money terms. Inflation reduces what that money is worth, so the real burden of the debt falls.
