## What it is
The rest of the map explains what things are and how the system works. This branch is about the thinking you bring to it. It gathers a small set of ideas, often called **mental models**, that apply to almost every money decision, whatever the asset.

The ideas fall into three groups:
- **Decisions:** looking past the first consequence to the next one, counting what you give up as well as what you get, asking whether you have a real advantage, telling luck from skill, matching choices to how long you can wait, and leaving room for being wrong.
- **Incentives and systems:** people respond to what they're rewarded for, and some costs fall on people who never chose them.
- **Thinking with numbers:** an average can hide what matters, "twice the risk" can mean almost nothing, and a precise-looking figure can be built on guesswork.

## Why it exists
Most costly money mistakes aren't caused by missing facts. They come from how the facts are weighed: chasing a recent winner, ignoring a cost because it's invisible, trusting a number because it has a decimal point. Professionals learn these habits of mind through training and through losses. This branch sets them out plainly, so anyone can use them on any page of the map.

## How it works
Each idea here is a question to ask, not a formula to apply:
- **And then what?** Most choices have effects that arrive later, and some of those reverse the first effect.
- **Compared with what?** Every use of money is also a choice not to use it some other way.
- **Why would I know better?** If there's no clear reason you have an advantage, assume you don't.
- **Skill, or luck?** A good result from a bad decision, or a bad result from a good one, is common over short periods.
- **Over what time?** The right answer often changes with how long you can wait.
- **What if I'm wrong?** A choice that only works if every estimate is right leaves no room for error.
- **Who gains?** Incentives explain much of what people sell, recommend and report.
- **What does the whole spread look like?** An average, a percentage or a single figure rarely tells the full story.

## What goes wrong
- **Using a model to confirm what you already believe.** Any of these ideas can be bent to justify a decision already made.
- **Applying one model to everything.** Each tool fits some problems and misleads on others. That's why there are several.
- **Thinking without facts.** Good reasoning on bad information still goes wrong. Check the numbers first, then think about them.
- **Over-thinking small choices.** Careful thought is worth most where a mistake would be large or hard to undo.

> **Key rule:** before any important money decision, ask what happens next, what you give up, and what happens if you're wrong.

## Check yourself

**Q:** A fund has beaten the market for three years in a row. What question from this branch should you ask before treating that as proof of skill?

**A:** Whether it could be luck. Over a few years, many funds will beat the market by chance alone, and the ones that did are the ones you hear about.

**Q:** You keep money in a current account paying no interest "to be safe". What does the idea of opportunity cost add to that choice?

**A:** That safety has a price: what the money could have earned elsewhere, and what inflation takes from it each year. That may still be the right choice, but it isn't free.

## Going deeper
- **Inversion:** instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid it.
- **Base rates:** before judging a single case, ask how often things like it usually work out.
- **Circle of competence:** knowing where your knowledge ends matters as much as what you know.
