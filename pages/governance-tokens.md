## What it is
A **governance token** gives its holders votes over a protocol: how it charges fees, how its treasury is spent, and how its code changes. Many [[defi]] protocols issue one.

## Why it matters
Governance tokens are the nearest crypto gets to shares, but they usually aren't shares. Most give no legal ownership and no automatic right to profits. Their value depends on what the votes can do, and on whether holders expect a share of fees in future.

## How it works
- **One token, one vote** is the usual rule, so large holders decide most outcomes.
- **Proposals** are posted, voted on, and in many protocols carried out automatically by code.
- **Treasuries** controlled by votes can be large, which gives the votes real economic weight.

## What goes wrong
- **Concentration:** founders, early funds and a few large holders often control most votes.
- **Low turnout:** few holders vote, so small, organised groups can pass proposals.
- **Governance attacks:** borrowing tokens to swing a vote, then returning them.
- **Unclear legal status:** whether such tokens are securities is unsettled in many places.

> **Key rule:** a vote is worth what it controls. Check who holds most of the votes and what they can change.

## Check yourself

**Q:** How is a typical governance token different from a share in a company?

**A:** A share gives legal ownership and a claim on profits after creditors. A governance token usually gives votes over a protocol but no legal ownership and no automatic right to its income.
