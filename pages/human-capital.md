## What it is
**Human capital** is the value of what you can do: your skills, knowledge, health, energy and judgment. Economists measure it as the present value of the income those abilities are likely to earn over your working life. The term was made popular by the economist Gary Becker in the 1960s.

## Why it matters
- **It's usually most of your wealth when you're young.** A new graduate may have little money but decades of earnings ahead.
- **It turns into the other stores.** Over a career, wages saved become deposits, bonds, shares and property.
- **It can rebuild from zero.** A trained engineer who loses every asset still has the skill that earned them.

## How it grows
- **Learning:** study, practice and on-the-job experience.
- **Health:** sleep, fitness and avoiding injury. A skill you can't use because you are ill earns nothing.
- **Choosing what to learn:** skills that are scarce and in demand earn more, and skills that transfer between employers or industries are safer.

## What goes wrong
- **Death or incapacity**, the killer on the [[cartography-of-wealth|Cartography of Wealth]]. It can't be transferred, so it ends with you.
- **Obsolescence:** technology or markets stop paying for a skill.
- **One-industry exposure:** a career built on a single employer or sector carries that sector's risk.

> **Key rule:** human capital is protected by insurance, by health and by learning skills that transfer.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why do people buy disability insurance?

**A:** Because it converts some of their human capital, which they can't otherwise protect or sell, into money if they can no longer work.
