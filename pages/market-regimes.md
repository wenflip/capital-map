## What it is
A **market regime** is a period in which the economy and markets behave in a recognisable way, and assets respond to the same few forces. The same portfolio can feel safe in one regime and dangerous in the next, because the relationships between assets change.

## Why it matters
Correlations, volatility and returns measured in one regime can mislead in another. Recognising which conditions you're in, and which you're not prepared for, is part of building a portfolio that survives.

## How it works
**Risk-on and risk-off.** The simplest split:
- **Risk-on:** money is easy to borrow, investors are confident, and shares, credit, crypto and other risky assets tend to rise together.
- **Risk-off:** fear dominates, investors sell risky assets and buy safe ones (cash, short-term government debt), and many risky assets fall together.

**Liquidity drives the switch.** When central banks cut rates or buy assets, and credit is easy, there's more money looking for returns. When they raise rates or shrink their balance sheets, that money pulls back. See [[central-bank-money]] and [[liquidity]].

**Growth and inflation.** A common way to map regimes uses two questions: is growth rising or falling, and is inflation rising or falling?

| | Inflation falling | Inflation rising |
|---|---|---|
| **Growth rising** | Usually good for shares | Often good for commodities and real assets |
| **Growth falling** | Often good for government bonds | Hard for shares and bonds alike |

The bottom-right corner, falling growth with rising inflation, is the one many portfolios are least ready for. 2022 came close to it: inflation surged, rates rose fast, and US shares and bonds fell together.

## What goes wrong
- **Assuming the last regime will last.** A portfolio built on decades of falling interest rates can struggle when rates rise.
- **Trying to time every switch.** Regimes are clearer in hindsight; frequent switching costs money and is often late.
- **One-regime portfolios.** A mix that only works when inflation is low is a single bet on inflation.

> **Key rule:** you don't need to predict the next regime. Hold a mix that can survive each of them.

## Check yourself

**Q:** Why can shares and government bonds fall together when inflation rises?

**A:** Because rising inflation pushes interest rates up. Higher rates lower bond prices directly, and they also lower the value of companies' future profits, so shares fall too.
