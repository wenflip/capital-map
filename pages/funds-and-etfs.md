## What it is
A **fund** pools money from many investors and buys a portfolio of shares, bonds or other assets. Each investor owns units of the fund, so one purchase gives you a slice of everything it holds.
- A traditional **mutual fund** (in some countries a **unit trust** or **OEIC**) is bought from and sold back to the fund company, once a day, at the fund's **net asset value** (NAV): the value of its holdings divided by its units.
- An **ETF** (exchange-traded fund) trades on a stock exchange like a share, at prices that change through the day.

## Why it exists
- **Diversification in one step.** Buying hundreds of companies one by one would be expensive and slow.
- **Small amounts.** You can own a global portfolio with a modest sum.
- **Choice of approach:**
  - **Index funds** copy an [[indices|index]] and charge low fees.
  - **Active funds** pay a manager to pick investments and try to beat the index, for higher fees.

## How it works
**How an ETF's price stays close to its holdings.** Large dealers called **authorised participants** can create new ETF units by delivering the underlying shares to the fund, or redeem units for the shares. If the ETF's price drifts above its NAV, they create units and sell them; if it falls below, they buy units and redeem them. That trade pulls the price back towards NAV.

**Fees compound against you.** A fund's yearly fee comes off its return every year. Fictional example: 10,000 invested for 30 years.

| Yearly return after fees | Value after 30 years |
|---|---|
| 7% (a low-cost fund) | 76,123 |
| 6% (the same fund with 1% more in fees) | 57,435 |

One percentage point a year costs 18,688, about a quarter of the final value. See [[compounding]].

**Active against index.** S&P's SPIVA scorecards compare active funds with their benchmark indices. Over the 15 years to December 2024, there was no category of US equity fund in which most active managers beat their benchmark.

## What goes wrong
- **High fees** quietly take a large share of long-run returns.
- **Narrow or complex funds.** Some ETFs hold a small niche, use borrowing, or reset daily. Their long-run returns can differ greatly from what the name suggests.
- **Thin trading.** In stressed markets, a small ETF's price can move away from the value of its holdings.
- **The fund is a claim.** You own units in the fund, held through a broker, not the assets directly. See [[claims-and-liabilities]].

> **Key rule:** in funds, the cost is the one thing you know in advance. Start with low fees and broad holdings, and make anything else earn its extra cost.

## Check yourself

**Q:** An ETF trades at 101 while the shares it holds are worth 100 per unit. What brings the price back down?

**A:** Authorised participants buy the underlying shares, deliver them to the fund for new ETF units, and sell those units at 101. The extra selling pushes the ETF's price back towards 100.
